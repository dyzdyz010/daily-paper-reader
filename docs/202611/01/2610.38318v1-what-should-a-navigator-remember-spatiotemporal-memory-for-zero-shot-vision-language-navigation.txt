## ABSTRACT 

Zero-shot vision-and-language navigation (VLN) requires an agent to use past observations without navigation-specific training. We study what information an 8B-scale navigation system should retain and how it should be organized for sub-sequent decisions. Our agent combines instruction progress, hierarchically com-pressed observation history anchored to poses, and accumulated geometry in com-plementary task, temporal, and spatial memories. An 8B observer summarizes observations, and an 8B reasoner uses the memories to select waypoint intents or request additional views, a geometric controller executes the selected targets. A semantic information perspective motivates the distinction between retaining ev-idence, making it accessible to the reasoner, and acquiring further observations. On R2R-CE val-unseen, the complete system achieves the highest among the eval-uated Qwen3-based zero-shot baselines. Removing temporal memory reduces SR and sharply increases average path length from 16.9 to 39.1 m, removing the spatial-map and planning component reduces SR as well. Real-world experiments on a Unitree Go2 show that our zero-shot method achieves higher success rates than the post-trained baseline in both corridor and office environments, suggesting better transfer to the evaluated real-world settings. As a complementary evalua-tion, LAW2 uses paired instruction interventions to examine behavioral responses, instruction following ability beyond endpoint success. These results support pre-serving task progress, observation history, and geometry in structured memory for zero-shot navigation with smaller open models. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Vision-and-Language Navigation (VLN) requires an embodied agent to follow a natural-language instruction through a previously unseen environment, providing a foundation for higher-level tasks such as object manipulation and human–robot interaction. Post-training methods (Guo et al., 2026; Wei et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026) fine-tune vision-language models on navigation trajectories and achieve strong success rates on R2R-CE (Krantz et al., 2020). Zero-shot methods (Chen et al., 2025; Shi et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026a) instead reuse pretrained models without navigation-specific training. Despite their different training requirements and inference resources, both approaches must integrate visual observations, instruction progress, and execution history to make reliable navigation decisions. A central question is how navigation history should be represented for smaller open models. In our reproduced HSGM configuration (Li et al., 2026a), replacing GPT-5 with Qwen3-VL-8B reduces SR from 0.463 to 0.049 on R2R-CE val-unseen (Table 1). This comparison motivates examining the demands placed on the navigation backbone. We focus on memory representation: the reasoner must connect current observations with instruction progress and earlier evidence, often across many actions. Our design preserves three complementary kinds of information: what the instruction requires and which subgoal is active, what the agent has observed and executed, and what geometry it has ex-1arXiv:2610.38318v1 [cs.RO] 28 Oct 2026 Preprint plored. We organize these as task memory, temporal memory with hierarchical summaries anchored to poses, and spatial memory containing an occupancy map and a committed target. The semantic communication perspective of Lastras et al. (2026) motivates retaining evidence use-ful for task-relevant judgments and organizing it for a computationally bounded receiver. In nav-igation, these judgments include whether a subgoal is complete and where to move next. We use this perspective as a design rationale. An 8B observer constructs observational summaries, while a Qwen3-VL-8B reasoner combines them with the instruction and spatial memory. Waypoint intents connect these decisions to geometric control, and a PREVIEW action allows the agent to acquire additional views. We evaluate this memory design through navigation results, component ablations, and deployment on a Unitree Go2 to experiment real world performance. We also introduce LAW2 as a complemen-tary diagnostic benchmark: paired direction reversals, route-information removal, and paraphrases reveal how behavior responds to instruction changes with the same meaning. Our contributions are as follows. • We propose a spatiotemporal memory representation for zero-shot VLN that combines in-struction progress, pose-anchored observation history, and accumulated geometry. • We evaluate the representation in a navigation system with an 8B observer and an 8B rea-soner. Component ablations characterize the effects of temporal memory, spatial memory and active previewing with planning, an integration with CA-Nav and real-world deploy-ment provide additional evidence of utility. • We introduce LAW2 to complement navigation metrics with paired tests of directional responsiveness, dependence on route information, and sensitivity to paraphrasing. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

2.1 VISION -AND -L ANGUAGE NAVIGATION 

R2R (Anderson et al., 2018) studies navigation from natural-language instructions on a discrete graph, while R2R-CE (Krantz et al., 2020) extends the task to continuous environments with low-level actions. REVERIE (Qi et al., 2020) additionally requires identifying an object referred to by the instruction. These settings require agents to connect language with visual observations and spatial decisions. Spatial representations are a central part of VLN systems. ETPNav (An et al., 2024) uses online topological mapping and obstacle-aware planning, while BEVBERT (An et al., 2022) combines global topological and local metric representations. Post-training approaches (Guo et al., 2026; Wei et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026) adapt pretrained models using navigation data. Our work studies how observation history and geometry can support navigation when the observer and reasoner are used without navigation-specific training. 2.2 AGENTIC NAVIGATION AND MEMORY 

Agentic VLN systems combine language or vision-language models with perception, planning, and execution modules (Xin et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2026a;b; Li et al., 2026b). FreeAskWorld (Peng et al., 2026) extends evaluation to dynamic environments and proactive tasks which needs robot to search more information when lack of task-relevant information. These settings motivate retaining information across successive decisions. Existing systems use different representations and interfaces for navigation. CA-Nav (Chen et al., 2025) uses a value map to score locations by their relevance to the instruction, and BSC-Nav (Ruan et al., 2026) explores brain-inspired spatial memory. We evaluate our approach alongside zero-shot systems including SmartWay, Open-Nav, and HSGM (Shi et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026a). Our focus is the organization of complementary evidence: task progress, compressed obser-vation history tied to poses, and accumulated geometry. The experiments assess this representation within a complete navigation system. 2Preprint 2.3 SPATIAL AND TEMPORAL MEMORY FOR NAVIGATION 

Memory representations for VLN organize both observation history and spatial structure. HAMT (Chen et al., 2021) hierarchically encodes panoramic observations and their temporal re-lationships for multimodal action prediction. GridMM (Wang et al., 2023) projects historical obser-vations into a unified spatial grid and aggregates visual evidence according to instruction relevance. These approaches establish the importance of structured history and spatially organized evidence. Our work examines how such information can be represented explicitly for pretrained observers and reasoners used without navigation-specific training. Recent VLM-based navigation systems further explore explicit memory representations. Map-Nav (Zhang et al., 2025) uses annotated semantic maps in place of historical frames, while HSGM (Li et al., 2026a) organizes geometric, semantic, and decision information to support subtask reasoning and waypoint planning. HAM-VLN (Liu et al., 2026) maintains a grounded world graph with decision-coupled semantic and reflective records, combining a recent-history window with subgoal-conditioned retrieval. These methods demonstrate that map construction, task progress, and selective history access are already important elements of navigation memory. ## 3 METHODOLOGY 

3.1 PROBLEM FORMULATION 

We consider vision-and-language navigation in a partially observable environment. An agent re-ceives a natural-language instruction G at the beginning of an episode and subsequently acquires observations while executing actions. We model the interaction as a POMDP Kaelbling et al. (1998), whose state st includes the environment layout and the agent’s physical configuration. The agent does not observe the complete state; instead, it receives observations vt = ( It, D t, P t), comprising an RGB image, depth, and a measured or estimated pose. 3Preprint The environment evolves according to

Let QG denote the task requirements associated with G. Successful execution requires the resulting trajectory to satisfy QG. Standard benchmark SR evaluates the narrower condition of stopping within a prescribed distance of the goal and does not by itself establish compliance with all route instructions. We therefore evaluate navigation performance together with instruction responsiveness. Appendix C develops the general receiver-based perspective underlying this formulation, distin-guishing available information, realized computation, and task achievement through environment interaction. 3.2 DESIGN RATIONALE 

Our memory design is motivated by the semantic information perspective of Lastras et al. (2026), which studies communication that enables a receiver to derive required conclusions using its exist-ing knowledge. For navigation, this motivates retaining evidence that supports task-relevant judg-ments rather than reproducing the entire observation stream. We adopt this perspective as a design rationale, without transferring its communication bounds directly to memory cost or navigation per-formance. We distinguish evidence availability from its effective use. A navigation decision may fail because the necessary evidence has not been observed, because it was discarded during memory compres-sion, or because the reasoner cannot reliably interpret and combine the retained information. These possibilities motivate complementary mechanisms: acquiring additional observations, retaining rel-evant history, and organizing that history into accessible representations. The agent is not assumed to identify these failure sources perfectly. We instantiate this rationale through task, temporal, and spatial memory. Task memory retains the instruction and its execution stage; temporal memory organizes past observations and measured mo-tion; spatial memory accumulates geometric evidence for navigation. Hierarchical summarization and pose anchoring organize historical information for the reasoner, while a preview mechanism allows the agent to request additional views. These mechanisms support memory-conditioned deci-sions without guaranteeing evidence sufficiency or correct reasoning. Appendix C.1 formalizes the distinction between semantic sufficiency and realized computation. ## 4 EVALUATION PROTOCOL 

We evaluate navigation on R2R-CE (Krantz et al., 2020) using success rate (SR), success weighted by path length (SPL), navigation error (NE), oracle success rate (OSR), and normalized dynamic time warping (nDTW) (Ilharco et al., 2019). We also report relative success rate, RSR = SR /OSR ,when OSR is positive and both metrics use the same cohort with complete oracle-success coverage. RSR measures successful stopping conditional on reaching the goal region; it does not separately identify perception, progress-estimation, or stopping errors. LAW2 complements these metrics with paired instruction interventions. Each transformed instruc-tion is compared with its original counterpart under the same scene, start pose, and execution set-tings: • FLIP reverses the initial turn command in 82 episodes to measure responsiveness to the supplied direction. • GOAL-ONLY retains the final annotated sub-instruction in 174 episodes to measure de-pendence on route information. Success remains defined by the original target location. • PARAPHRASE generates four intended meaning-preserving variants for each of 200 in-structions: same register, terse, colloquial, and landmark re-description. ## 5 EXPERIMENTS 

5.1 SIMULATION EXPERIMENT 

Our method uses JoyAI-VL-Interaction-8B (Yao et al., 2026) as the observer, which summarizes visual observations into temporal memory, and Qwen3-VL-8B (Bai et al., 2025) as the reasoner. We compare against both post-training and zero-shot baselines. The zero-shot baselines differ in how they use the LLM; for example, CA-Nav uses it only for subgoal decomposition. For the Qwen3-based comparisons, we retain each baseline’s implementation and use the backbone configurations listed in Table 1. These comparisons share an 8B-scale language backbone but differ in auxiliary models, observation configurations, and action interfaces. All experiments run on a server with 8 NVIDIA A100 GPUs. 5.2 REAL -W ORLD EXPERIMENT 

We deploy our method and AwareVLN on a Unitree Go2 quadruped robot in corridor and office en-vironments. Both models run on the same A100 server, which communicates with the Go2 remotely. The robot streams its onboard observations to the server and executes the returned actions. We re-construct each environment with FAST-LIVO2 (Zheng et al., 2024) and localize the robot within the reconstructed map, which provides the pose ˆPt during navigation and the reference positions used for evaluation. 5.3 MAIN RESULTS 

Table 1 shows that our complete system achieves SR of 0.263 and SPL of 0.196, the highest among the evaluated Qwen3-based zero-shot baselines. Post-trained models achieve higher SR and SPL in simulation. Our RSR of 0.802 indicates that a larger fraction of episodes reaching the goal region end successfully than for the other evaluated Qwen3-based zero-shot methods. This comparison concerns complete systems with different perception modules and action interfaces. Adding temporal memory to CA-Nav increases SPL from 0.097 to 0.126 and nDTW from 0.162 to 0.225, while SR sligtly changes from 0.243 to 0.247. Average path length decreases from 27.82 to 23.50 m. These results provide additional evidence that retained observation history can improve path efficiency, although the change in endpoint success is small. 8Preprint In the two real-world environments, our system obtains higher SR and lower NE than AwareVLN(Table 4). This result demonstrate a better generalization ability that our method have. Figure 2 is the qualitative results which illustrates simulation and real-world trajectories and task relevant elements visualization. 5.4 ABLATION STUDY 

Table 2 reports the ablation results. We evaluate the proposed memory designs by replacing each component with a minimal baseline. For w/o Temporal , the reasoner receives a sliding window of the eight most recent raw images, without historical observation reports and hierarchical summaries. For w/o Spatial , the agent retains only its pose trajectory and the currently committed target. Way-point candidates are generated from the current observatio, and the agent moves directly toward the selected target without an accumulated occupancy map. The w/o S&T variant combines both replacements. Task memory is retained in all variants, and preview remains enabled in the memory ablations. Removing temporal memory reduces SR from 0.263 to 0.190, SPL from 0.196 to 0.112, and nDTW from 0.379 to 0.201. Average path length increases from 16.9 to 39.1 m and steps from 131 to 292, even though OSR rises from 0.328 to 0.475. The agent therefore reaches the goal region more frequently during longer trajectories but converts fewer visits into successful termination. This pattern is consistent with historical evidence supporting task progress and stopping decisions. Removing the spatial component reduces SR to 0.205 and SPL to 0.142. Removing both spatial and temporal components yields SR of 0.185 and nDTW of 0.202. Task memory is retained in every variant, so these comparisons assess temporal and spatial components within the task-conditioned architecture. Removing PREVIEW reduces SR from 0.263 to 0.190 while leaving nDTW nearly unchanged (0.379 versus 0.378). Token consumption also decreases. This tradeoff supports the utility of acquiring additional views within our system. Separately, the intent-action variant achieves higher SR and SPL than the primitive-action variant (0.263/0.196 versus 0.125/0.085), supporting the use of waypoint selection and geometric control to execute memory-conditioned decisions. 5.5 INSTRUCTION -I NTERVENTION ANALYSIS 

In FLIP, our initial-turn follow rate changes from 0.829 to 0.707, compared with 0.915 to 0.439 for AwareVLN and 0.646 to 0.268 for CA-Nav (Table 5). Our system has the highest mean follow rate across the paired conditions. These results show differences in responsiveness to a changed direction command on the selected episodes. In GOAL-ONLY, our SR decreases from 0.293 to 0.181, while nDTW increases from 0.354 to 0.390 and path length decreases from 19.37 to 11.25 m (Table 6). AwareVLN shows decreases in both SR and nDTW, whereas CA-Nav shows lower SR with a small increase in nDTW. Thus, route-information removal affects endpoint success and original-route fidelity differently, and the zero-shot method tends to act like a human to explore with higher nDTW. Under PARAPHRASE, our SR ranges from 0.240 to 0.280 around the original value of 0.270, while nDTW decreases from 0.387 to 0.350–0.365 across all four variants (Table 7). Endpoint success is therefore relatively stable in the reported point estimates, whereas route fidelity changes. For AwareVLN, A4 reduces SR from 0.590 to 0.500 and nDTW from 0.621 to 0.566, indicating sensitivity to landmark re-description. 

## 6 CONCLUSION 

We studied what information a zero-shot navigator should retain when using 8B-scale models. Our design combines instruction progress, pose-anchored observation history, and accumulated geome-try in complementary memories. The complete system achieves the highest SR and SPL among the evaluated Qwen3-based zero-shot baselines, and the ablations show the utility of temporal history and spatial mapping with planning. Temporal-memory integration with CA-Nav and deployment on a Unitree Go2 for real world experiment provide further evidence of practical utility. Testing the initial turn avoids conditioning evaluation on arrival at an intermediate intervention lo-cation. An episode is eligible when: 1. ## C A S EMANTIC INFORMATION PERSPECTIVE ON EMBODIED AI 

We describe how a receiver combines a communicated task message with its existing information, selects actions, and acquires further observations through environment interaction. The formula-tion separates information available for reasoning, realized computation, and satisfaction of a task specification. C.1 AVAILABLE INFORMATION AND RECEIVER COMPUTATION 

Lastras et al. (2026) study communication that enables a receiver to prove logical conclusions using its existing knowledge. Inspired by this receiver-centered perspective, we consider an initial mes-sage G that communicates task requirements and may contain descriptive information. We model the receiver’s computation explicitly and use a POMDP to describe its subsequent environment in-teraction. This formulation does not establish new communication bounds or assume that logical communication limits directly characterize task performance. C.1.1 RECEIVER MODEL 

We describe a receiver R through four elements: its internal configuration θ, current internal in-formation state ht, information-processing mechanism M, and output-selection settings λt. These specify relatively stable parameters, retained information available for the current computation, com-putational operations, and rules for selecting an output. This is a functional decomposition; it does not prescribe a particular architecture or memory organization.
