ABSTRACT 

Multi-agent LLM systems increasingly rely on persistent memory and inter-agent communication. These capabilities create privacy risks that extend beyond indi-vidual interactions: a release appropriate in isolation may become inappropriate when combined with information previously disclosed to a recipient or propagated to downstream agents along the planned workflow. Existing Contextual Integrity (CI) mechanisms for agentic systems typically rely on local judgments that assess each disclosure in isolation, with or without regard to the recipient’s history, but do not account for how information propagates downstream. However, compliance at the current boundary does not guarantee appropriateness across the workflow. We introduce Task-Graph Contextual Integrity (TG-CI) , to our knowledge the first task-graph-native formalization of Contextual Integrity for multi-agent sys-tems. TG-CI represents a workflow as a Multi-Agent Task Graph (MATG) 

and extends CI from atomic transfers to stateful, graph-level information flows by jointly considering an item’s intended propagation scope and recipients’ evolving disclosure states. We operationalize TG-CI through propagation-scoped autho-rization under two complementary policies: Full-Scope , which requires admissi-bility across the complete downstream scope, and Auto-Region , which confines each item to its maximal admissible region using lightweight frontier gates. We further introduce MAPLE-Bench , a benchmark of 144 cases across four scenarios that jointly vary MATG topology, disclosure history, compromised-agent place-ment, and item-level privacy policies. In our primary experimental configuration, TG-CI reduces the mean unauthorized disclosure count by 85 .8–94 .1% relative to the Local-History CI baseline, while retaining 93 .6–96 .1% of the baseline’s task accuracy, with the two enforcement policies providing complementary privacy– cost–utility trade-offs. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Multi-agent systems increasingly combine persistent memory with task delegation and inter-agent communication to solve complex problems (Guo et al., 2024; Tran et al., 2025). While these capa-bilities improve coordination and continuity, they also enlarge the privacy attack surface: sensitive information retrieved by one agent can be retained (Lahjouji & Colaco, 2026), combined with prior knowledge (Patil et al., 2025), and propagated to other agents throughout the workflow (Lahjouji & Colaco, 2026; Bhosale et al., 2026). Figure 1 illustrates three primary privacy violation modes in such systems. This risk is especially concerning when some agents are compromised. For in-stance, while disclosing medical appointment metadata to a scheduling service may be permissible in isolation, aggregating this with preexisting recipient data might expose sensitive clinical details, which may then propagate to a compromised downstream notification agent. In such settings, pro-tecting an individual memory access in isolation is insufficient, because the privacy consequences of a disclosure depend also on prior disclosure and the downstream multi-agent workflow. Contextual Integrity (CI) provides a natural foundation for privacy in multi-agent workflows (Chen et al., 2025), yet how downstream task structure should inform history-aware disclosure judgments remains underexplored. CI evaluates whether an information flow is appropriate within its surround-ing context (Nissenbaum, 2004; Barth et al., 2006), rather than relying solely on fixed sensitivity labels. The AgentSCOPE (Ngong et al., 2026) benchmark further expands it to multi-tool agentic workflow by labeling internal chan-nels but still considers each information flow independently and provides no defense. Recent work has begun to address cumulative disclosure risks through trajectory-level privacy budgeting (Xie & Li, 2026). Meanwhile, empirical studies identify inter-agent communication as a major channel of privacy leakage in multi-agent systems (Yagoubi et al., 2026), yet comparatively little work consid-ers how the downstream task structure itself should influence an earlier disclosure decision. This leaves a central question underexplored: how can we extend contextual integrity to evaluate an in-formation flow by jointly accounting for direct, cumulative, and transitive disclosure risks within a multi-agent task structure? 

To address this gap, we introduce Task-Graph Contextual Integrity (TG-CI) , operationalize it through propagation-scoped authorization with dual-mode enforcement, and develop MAPLE-Bench for controlled evaluation of multi-agent privacy. TG-CI uses a Multi-Agent Task Graph (MATG) to evaluate a current information flow in light of recipients’ disclosure histories and in-tended downstream propagation, jointly accounting for direct, cumulative, and transitive disclosure risks. To make these judgments effective beyond the release boundary, the runtime binds each re-leased item to an authorized propagation scope, allowing either conservative admission or selective downstream containment. MAPLE-Bench jointly varies task structure, recipient disclosure history, and compromised-agent placement to assess whether these controls limit all three disclosure risks while preserving task utility. In summary, our contributions are: (1) Task-graph-native contextual integrity. We formalize TG-CI, to our knowledge the first task-graph-native CI formalism that judges a current disclosure jointly with its intended downstream propagation scope and recipients’ evolving disclosure states. TG-CI preserves classical CI se-mantics at each atomic transfer while lifting the judgment to a graph-level information flow. (2) Propagation-scoped authorization and dual-mode enforcement. We operationalize each positive TG-CI decision as a propagation-scoped authorization and introduce two complemen-tary enforcement policies. Full-Scope Mode blocks an item if any transfer in its complete downstream closure is inadmissible, whereas Auto-Region Authorization computes the max-2Preprint imal rooted admissible region and removes the item at its propagation frontier. This design avoids expensive and repeated contextual re-evaluation along downstream routes while offering complementary trade-offs among privacy protection, computational cost, and task utility. (3) A benchmark for multi-agent privacy. We release MAPLE-Bench (Multi-Agent Privacy Leakage Evaluation Benchmark), a reusable benchmark of 144 cases spanning four applica-tion scenarios, diverse MATG topologies, and compromised-agent configurations. Each case provides item-level memory, privacy policies and ground-truth annotations for disclosure viola-tions, enabling controlled evaluation of information exposure and propagation containment. 

2 PROBLEM SETTING 

System model. We consider a multi-agent system consisting of a set of agents V that collabora-tively execute a user task using persistent memory and inter-agent communication. At execution, we represent the current workflow as a directed, labeled Multi-Agent Task Graph (MATG) 

G = ( V, E, τ, π ), (1) where E ⊆ V × V contains planned task-dependent delegation or information-transfer relations, 

τ (v) denotes the current subtask assigned to agent v, and π(e) denotes the edge transmission rule associated with edge e. The MATG differs from a communication-connectivity graph: (u, v ) ∈ E

indicates that the current workflow anticipates a task-relevant interaction from u to v, not merely that the two agents are technically able to communicate. 

Memory model. Agents access a persistent memory system M that stores information accumu-lated from interactions. We model a memory record as a set of information items mi = ⟨di, a i, x i⟩,where di is the data subject, ai the information attribute or type, and xi its value or content. This granularity matches the subject and attribute parameters of CI (Barth et al., 2006) and the field-level user data in prior CI evaluations of agents (Bagdasarian et al., 2024; Shao et al., 2024). During task execution, an agent v ∈ V may issue a memory query qv and receive a retrieved subset R(qv ) ⊆ M .The formulation does not depend on the memory architecture: M may be shared, agent-specific, or hierarchical, as in current agent memory systems (Packer et al., 2024; Chhikara et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). We only require that every memory access goes through a system-mediated interface. Access-controlled shared memory makes the same requirement (Rezazadeh et al., 2025). 

Adversary model. An unknown subset C ⊆ V with |C| ≤ f may be compromised before exe-cution, as in prior studies of malicious agents in LLM multi-agent systems (tse Huang et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026). A compromised agent may deviate arbitrarily from its intended behavior: it can issue overbroad or adversarial memory requests, misrepresent task necessity, and transform sensi-tive content in inter-agent messages. The adversary observes all information delivered to agents in 

C and seeks to maximize the protected information learned by the coalition and the inappropriate flows in the system. We assume that adversarial communication goes through the system-mediated memory and messaging interfaces, as in information-flow control for agents (Debenedetti et al., 2025; Costa et al., 2025). Out-of-band channels and covert encodings in permitted messages, such as steganographic collusion (Motwani et al., 2024), are outside our threat model. 

Privacy objective. Let Dt(v) summarize the privacy-relevant information delivered to recipient 

v through monitored channels before execution step t (formalized in Section 3). Given the MATG 

G and disclosure states Dt, we seek a CI-based judgment of whether an item mi may flow from a source s ∈ V ∪ {M} to a recipient r0 ∈ V under an intended propagation scope P :

J(mi, s, r 0, P | G, D t) ∈ { 0, 1},

where J = 1 denotes admissibility. An admissible flow must not cause direct, cumulative, or tran-sitive disclosure violations within its intended propagation scope P . The objective is to prevent inappropriate exposure and propagation while supporting task-relevant information use, not to en-sure secrecy from compromised agents when access is contextually justified.

4 OPERATIONALIZATION 

Architecture overview. Our implementation treats TG-CI as a binary judge and separates it from the mechanisms used to construct and enforce a propagation region. For each memory item mi, the disclosure controller returns either (RELEASE , P i), where Pi is the authorized propagation scope, or 

(BLOCK , ∅). We instantiate this controller in two modes, as shown in Figure 2. Full-Scope Mode evaluates the item over the complete downstream MATG, whereas Auto-Region Mode constructs the maximal rooted region through which the item remains admissible. Both modes use the same TG-CI formalism and differ only in how Pi is selected. We assume that the MATG remains fixed throughout each task execution and leave supporting dynamic replanning to future work. 

Item-level authorization and exposure-state tracking. A memory response is first decomposed into semantic items R(qr0 ) = {m1, . . . , m n}, following the memory representation in Section 2. For the disclosure state Dt, the implementation maintains a conservative authorization state Dt (Ap-pendix A), where Dt(v) includes information actually delivered to v and items currently authorized to reach v. Each memory response is evaluated using a transaction-local state bD initialized from 

Dt. Whenever mi is tentatively authorized over Pi, it is added to bD(v) for every v ∈ V (Pi) before the next item is evaluated. This captures cumulative disclosure both within the current response and across previously authorized flows. If the response is authorized, the tentative updates are committed to Dt; otherwise, they are discarded.

5 EXPERIMENTS 

5.1 MAPLE-B ENCH 

Motivation for a complementary benchmark. Recent benchmarks have expanded privacy evaluation beyond final outputs, but emphasize different dimensions of multi-agent leakage. AgentSCOPE localizes contextual violations across intermediate agent–tool flows (Ngong et al., 2026), while AgentLeak audits leakage through internal channels, including inter-agent messages and shared memory (Yagoubi et al., 2026). PiSAs annotates both task appropriateness and recip-ient visibility across agent topologies and memory regimes (Gupta et al., 2026); MAMA isolates the effect of network topology on adversarial memory extraction (Liu et al., 2026); and The Sum Leaks More Than Its Parts studies sensitive inferences formed by composing individually benign disclosures (Patil et al., 2025). These benchmarks establish important individual risk dimensions, but do not provide a single controlled setting that jointly varies MATG topology, recipient disclo-sure history, compromised-agent placement, and item-level propagation permissions while retaining an objective measure of task completion. MAPLE-Bench complements this literature by combining these factors within the same cases, enabling controlled evaluation of whether a defense can preserve task utility while preventing direct, cumulative, and transitive disclosure. 

Benchmark composition. MAPLE-Bench contains 144 cases across four application scenarios, with 36 cases per scenario. Each case instantiates a four-agent MATG and a terminal node rep-resenting the user or final-output recipient. Within each scenario, cases are evenly divided among chain, tree, and branching topologies. 108 cases contain one compromised agent and 36 contain two, with compromised-agent locations varied across the MATG. Each case contains 30 synthetic memory items with fictitious values: 12 agent-specific private items, with three associated with each task agent, and 18 shared items. Only seven items contribute to the answers of five multiple-choice questions, either individually or in combination; the remaining 23 items serve as distractors to model memory stores in which most recorded information is irrelevant to the current task, simulating the real-life scenario. The detail of the benchmark is provided in Appendix B. 

Privacy annotations and evaluation. Each case provides a task-conditioned item–recipient pol-icy matrix Y , where Yi,v = 1 iff recipient v may receive memory item mi for its assigned task. Policies cover all four agents and the terminal output recipient, providing ground truth for direct disclosures and for transitive violations. Cases targeting cumulative leakage additionally identify item combinations whose joint disclosure reveals a protected fact. Each benchmark instance there-fore specifies the MATG, agent subtasks, compromised-agent configuration, memory items, privacy policies, multiple-choice questions, and gold answers. Because all sensitive values are synthetic, MAPLE-Bench enables measurement of task accuracy, unauthorized and cumulative exposure, and propagation containment without using real personal data. 5.2 BASELINES AND EXPERIMENTAL CONDITIONS 

Baselines. We compare the two TG-CI enforcement modes against three baselines. No Protec-tion returns all retrieved memory items to the requesting agent and delivers inter-agent messages without privacy judgment or filtering. Local-History CI retains item-level judgment and recipient disclosure-state tracking, and evaluates each item over the initial memory-release edge. Public im-plementations of the closest cumulation-aware systems, OCELOT and CAMP, were unavailable at the time of evaluation (Xie & Li, 2026; Panjwani, 2026); we therefore use Local-History CI as a controlled in-framework comparison rather than claiming an exact reproduction. We also evaluate 

Online Atomic CI , which applies the same edge-level atomic CI used by TG-CI independently 6Preprint at every realized memory and inter-agent transfer. The proposed systems are evaluated as TG-CI Full-Scope and TG-CI Auto-Region .

Experimental conditions. We implement the multi-agent workflows using LangChain and use Mem0 as the persistent memory backend. Task agents perform memory retrieval, inter-agent com-munication, and final-answer generation, while a separate privacy judge evaluates item- and edge-level contextual admissibility. We evaluate three agent–judge model assignments: L UNA –L UNA ,HAIKU –H AIKU , and H AIKU –L UNA , where the two names denote the task-agent and privacy-judge models, respectively. GPT-5.6 Luna and Claude Haiku 4.5 are used as the underlying model fami-lies. Unless otherwise specified, all main-text results are reported for the H AIKU –L UNA assignment, with results for remaining agent–judge configurations provided in Appendix C. Frontier filtering is performed locally using MiniLM for semantic candidate retrieval and DeBERTa for bidirectional entailment verification. Details of this procedure are provided in Appendix D. 

Metrics. We report four run-level metrics. Unauthorized Disclosure Count is the number of delivery events in which a memory item reaches a recipient for whom it is inappropriate according to the benchmark policy; repeated disclosures are counted separately. For cumulative cases, the delivery that first completes an annotated prohibited item combination is counted as an unauthorized disclosure. Compromised-Agent Exposure Count is the number of distinct inappropriate item observed by compromised agents, counting repeated delivery of the same item to the same agent only once. Judge Execution Token Count is the total number of additional input and output tokens used by privacy-judge LLM calls; local MiniLM and DeBERTa inference is excluded. Utility Score

6 CONCLUSION 

We introduced Task-Graph Contextual Integrity (TG-CI) , a task-graph-native extension of Con-textual Integrity that evaluates protected disclosures as stateful information flows over a Multi-Agent Task Graph, jointly accounting for recipient disclosure histories and intended downstream propaga-tion. We operationalized TG-CI through propagation-scoped authorization with two complementary policies: Full-Scope Mode for conservative end-to-end protection and Auto-Region Mode for lower-cost frontier-based containment. We further introduced MAPLE-Bench, a controlled benchmark for direct, cumulative, and transitive privacy violations in multi-agent workflows. Our experiments show TG-CI effectively reduced unauthorized disclosure and compromised-agent exposure while retain-ing substantial task utility. A CONSERVATIVE DISCLOSURE STATES 

Let H1 ⪯ H2 mean that H1 contains no more privacy-relevant information than H2. Since the conservative authorization state includes both delivered information and items authorized to reach a recipient, we have Dt(v) ⪯ Dt(v).

Assumption (downward-closed admissibility). For a fixed atomic flow φe(a), acceptance under a larger disclosure footprint implies acceptance under every smaller footprint: 

H1 ⪯ H2 ∧ CI( φe(a) | H2) = 1 = ⇒ CI( φe(a) | H1) = 1 . (14) Under this assumption, evaluating against Dt(v), or its transaction-local extension bD(v), is conser-vative: acceptance also holds under any actual disclosure state bounded by the evaluated footprint. Undelivered authorizations may therefore cause additional rejections, but not false acceptances due solely to state overapproximation. This justification requires the footprint used for authorization to remain an upper bound on the recipient’s actual state at delivery.

E PROVENANCE -PRESERVING ABSTRACTIONS .

The core TG-CI formalism does not explicitly model arbitrary semantic transformations performed by intermediate agents. Let 

a′ ⪯ a (19) denote a provenance-preserving abstraction of a, meaning that a′ reveals no more protected infor-mation than a.We assume contextual admissibility is monotone under such abstractions:

A provenance-preserving abstrac-tion a′ ⪯ a may traverse any edge already authorized for a, but abstraction does not automatically authorize propagation outside P .Transformations that combine a with independent information, reveal a new protected attribute, or otherwise increase privacy-relevant information are not treated as abstractions under Eq. 20; they require a separate privacy-state update or TG-CI judgment.
