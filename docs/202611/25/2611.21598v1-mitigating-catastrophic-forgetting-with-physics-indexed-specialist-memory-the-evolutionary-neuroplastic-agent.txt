Abstract —Continual reinforcement learning requires adapta-tion to changing conditions while preserving performance under previously encountered dynamics. It was investigated whether specialists generated by evolutionary search can provide areusable memory when combined with environment detection and failure-driven search. The Evolutionary Neuroplastic Agent (ENA) evolves small neural networks without gradients, archives selected policies by detected dynamic regime, and retrieves them through Bayesian policy reuse. The architecture was evaluated in non-stationary CartPole and HalfCheetah environments. In the 30-replicate CartPole ablation, the complete architecture achieved a mean success rate of 83.2% across the five test environments. Among variants that learned the harder Cart-Pole environments, the complete architecture showed the most consistent retention. A selection ablation also showed that the retrieval rule affected performance. In the evaluated sequences, ENA adapted more slowly than PPO on CartPole and achieved lower returns than SAC on HalfCheetah. The evidence concerns the complete architecture and does not isolate gradient-free optimization as the cause of retention or establish superiority over other continual-learning methods. 

Index Terms —Continual Reinforcement Learning, Neuroevo-lution, Non-Stationary Environments, Change Detection, Policy Reuse 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Reinforcement learning (RL) agents are usually trained and deployed on the assumption that the environment does not change. When it does, for instance when a robot’s physics shift between conditions, an agent with a single policy adapts by overwriting the parameters that encoded its previous be-havior. This is catastrophic forgetting [1]. When the original conditions return, the agent has to learn them again. Continual reinforcement learning studies agents that keep adapting without losing what they have already learned [2]. One family of methods protects or partitions the weights of a shared network, by regularization [3], replay [4] or task-specific heads [5]. Another keeps a separate policy for each de-tected context and recalls it when that context returns [6]–[8]. Neuroevolution offers a third route. It trains neural networks by mutation and selection over a population of candidates, instead of by gradient descent. Nisioti et al. [9] showed that evolution strategies balance adapting to new tasks and remem-bering old ones well, while genetic algorithms learn new tasks best but forget more. In these methods, however, the whole population evolves toward the current task. The population receives no signal that the environment has changed, nor indication of which earlier solution to restore when an earlier environment returns. Evolutionary methods learn by trying many candidate solu-tions at once. Along the way, they produce many solutions that already work in the conditions they were tried in. The question addressed in this paper is whether a small set of skills can turn those solutions into a memory that mitigates forgetting. The skills are inspired by how organisms vary under stress and consolidate memories. The main research question is: 

RQ1 When an agent encounters a sequence of non-stationary environments without explicit change no-tifications, does preserving effective policies in-dexed by detected physical dynamics—combined with failure-driven modulation of search effort— mitigate catastrophic forgetting? Three narrower questions examine this memory mechanism in detail: 

RQ1a Is preserving successful policies sufficient on its own, or must the archive be explicitly indexed by the physical regime under which each policy was validated? 

RQ1b Does policy archiving require additional environ-ment interaction, or does it operate as an emergent by-product of population evaluations? 

RQ1c Can dynamic shifts and previously encountered regimes be autonomously detected and identified purely from unassisted agent experience? Searching incurs computational cost, as candidate policies re-quire evaluation in the environment. The secondary research question is therefore: 

RQ2 Does dynamically concentrating search effort dur-ing performance failure achieve competitive reten-tion and reliability while significantly reducing the arXiv:2611.21598v1 [cs.LG] 16 Nov 2026 computational cost incurred by uniform full-effort search? To address these questions, the Evolutionary Neuroplastic Agent (ENA) is introduced. ENA evolves a population of small neural networks, termed specialists . Each specialist is scored with a Trust metric tracking recent return history. Search effort, termed Plasticity , scales dynamically with the performance deficit of the active specialist. Validated spe-cialists are archived in a Hall of Fame . Environment detec-tion identifies dynamic transitions and recognizes previously encountered physical regimes strictly from unassisted agent observations. On CartPole, it evaluates predictive accuracy under transition models of each known regime. On HalfChee-tah, it applies a structured diagnostic perturbation—a chirp —and analyzes mechanical response features. Specialist selection 

governs which archived specialist to deploy via Bayesian policy reuse [10], integrating detector evidence with observed episodic returns. As a structural design constraint, no compo-nent of ENA computes gradients. Two domains are studied. In CartPole [11], the agent balances a pole on a moving cart. In HalfCheetah, a two-legged robot simulated in MuJoCo [12] learns to run. In each domain, the agent trains on three environments in turn, which differ in gravity and other physical parameters (Section IV). The evidence for the claim is a skill ablation : the same agent, with each skill switched off in turn. Alongside it, standard single-policy learners were trained on the same sequences of environments, with the same number of environment steps: DQN [13], PPO [14] and SAC [15]. They show how much standard learners forget on these streams. They are a reference, not opponents. It is not claimed that ENA outperforms other continual-RL methods , nor that gradient-free learning is what prevents forgetting. The question is only whether these skills mitigate it. Comparing ENA with continual-RL systems built for the same problem is left to future work (Section VI-D). The contributions, each tied to the question it answers, are:  

> •

A skill ablation on CartPole, which switches each skill off in turn (RQ1, RQ1a, RQ1b, RQ2).  

> •

A selection ablation , which compares Bayesian policy reuse with simpler ways of choosing an archived special-ist (RQ1a).  

> •

A measurement of forgetting in standard single-policy learners, on CartPole and HalfCheetah, as the reference for RQ1.  

> •

Gradient-free environment detection for both domains, including the chirp for HalfCheetah, evaluated on adetection benchmark (RQ1c).  

> •

A study of what failure-driven search costs : one run at full effort in every episode, and two alternative rules for turning failure into search effort (RQ2).  

> •

Reproducibility : every result and setting reported here is generated by scripts, from the released code and experiment outputs. Section II reviews related work. Section III states the prob-lem and describes ENA. Section IV presents the environments, protocols and baselines, and Section V the results. Section VI answers the research questions and discusses the limitations. Section VII concludes. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Non-Stationary and Continual Reinforcement Learning 

A neural network trained by gradient descent on one task after another degrades on the earlier tasks. McCloskey and Cohen named this failure catastrophic interference [1]. Con-tinual reinforcement learning studies agents that must keep learning in such conditions without losing what they already know [2]. Elastic Weight Consolidation [3] slows learning on the weights that mattered for earlier tasks. CLEAR [4] replays past experience with behavioral cloning, and needs no task boundaries. When tasks are incompatible, a single network cannot serve them all. For such tasks, OWL [5] gives each task its own policy head on a shared feature extractor, and chooses a head at test time with a multi-armed bandit. These three methods protect or partition the weights of one gradient-trained network. ENA, like the context-based methods below, preserves whole policies instead. An archived specialist is never trained again, so there is nothing to protect. 

B. Evolutionary Reinforcement Learning and Continual Neu-roevolution 

Neuroevolution optimizes neural networks by mutation and selection over a population, instead of by gradients [16]. Evolution strategies [17] and genetic algorithms [18] are competitive with gradient-based RL on standard benchmarks. They need many samples, however [19], [20], and optimizing many parameters remains hard for them [19]. Hybrid methods use a population to diversify the experience of a gradient-trained learner [19]. Nisioti et al. [9] compared evolution strategies and genetic algorithms with PPO, continual RL methods and population-based RL, under changes of task and environment. Evolution strategies achieved the best balance between adapting and remembering. Genetic algorithms were the most plastic, but forgot more. The authors related this balance to how much the neighborhoods of good solutions in weight space overlap between consecutive tasks. Earlier work on evolutionary algorithms for changing op-timization problems developed remedies such as increasing diversity after a change, maintaining diversity throughout the run, and memory [21]. ENA uses these three (Section III-C). In the methods Nisioti et al. study, as in standard neuroevolution, the population evolves as a whole toward the current task. Changes are not detected, and earlier solutions are not stored and recalled. ENA adds both without gradients: it identifies the dynamics online, and it preserves specialists indexed by their physics. 

C. Quality-Diversity and Repertoire-Based Adaptation 

Quality-Diversity algorithms such as MAP-Elites [22] search for many diverse, high-performing solutions instead of a single optimum, and store them in an archive. Intelligent Trial and Error (ITE) [23] computes such an archive offline, in simulation. With it, a damaged robot finds a working gait within minutes, by trying archived behaviors guided by a Bayesian model. TeLAPA [24] brings the same idea to continual RL. For each task, it trains a base policy with PPO and fills an archive of behaviorally diverse policies around it with MAP-Elites. When a new or revisited task begins, short adaptation probes pick a starting point from all archives, so that competent alternatives remain available when a task is revisited. TeLAPA is evaluated on MiniGrid and on cyclic friction shifts in MuJoCo, HalfCheetah included, with the task boundaries given. ENA’s Hall of Fame is also an archive of specialists, but it differs in two ways. It is built online, while the environment changes. It is organized by the physics the agent detects, not by a behavior descriptor. 

D. Change Detection and Policy Reuse 

Sequential change detection asks when a data stream has changed. Classic methods range from Page’s CUSUM [25] to Bayesian online changepoint detection [26]. On CartPole, ENA uses the Predictive-Mixture CuSum [27]. It detects a change away from a known distribution without a model of the distribution after the change, by mixing predictors fitted on recent windows of data. In RL, RL-CD [6] learned one model of the environment per 

context , and recognized contexts by which model predicts best. ENA follows this idea. MBCD [7] combined an ensemble of learned probabilistic dynamics models with a high-confidence change-point detector whose false-alarm rate is bounded. It created a new policy for each new context, and reused an earlier one when a context recurred. SWOKS [8] detects contexts with optimal-transport distances and Kolmogorov– Smirnov tests, and trains one policy per context. These systems are the closest to ENA in purpose, and they too preserve a dedicated policy per context. RL-CD is tabular, while MBCD trains its models and policies by gradient descent, and SWOKS its policies. ENA differs from these systems in how memory is popu-lated and recalled, rather than in representation. Its specialists are individuals generated inherently by population search, rendering policy archiving free of dedicated training overhead. The search that produces them is scaled to failure. Recall weighs the detector’s evidence together with the observed returns. Section VI-D explains why these systems are not baselines here. To choose which stored policy to deploy, Bayesian Policy Reuse [10] keeps a belief over task types. The belief is updated from a signal of each episode, such as its return or its transitions. ENA adapts it to an online, non-stationary setting, and updates the belief from the returns and the detector’s likelihoods together (Section III-I). 

E. Positioning 

Table I summarizes how related methods preserve and recall earlier behavior. OWL and TeLAPA are told the task boundaries during training, so they detect nothing online. ITE starts adapting when it senses a performance drop. TeLAPA is partly gradient-free: it trains with PPO, and fills its archives by mutation. ENA does not introduce any of these capabilities on its own. Change detection, recurrence recognition, whole-policy preservation, gradient-free learning and probing all have precedents. What ENA combines is threefold. Its archive is populated with candidate solutions generated naturally during evolutionary search (RQ1b). The archive is indexed online by physics the agent detects without labels (RQ1c). The search that feeds it scales its effort to failure (RQ2). No component computes a gradient. Unlike most methods in the table, ENA also assumes that it can reset the environment: to evaluate candidates and, on HalfCheetah, to play its chirp. III. M ETHODOLOGY 

A. Problem Setting 

The agent meets a sequence of environments M1, . . . , MK .Each is a Markov decision process Mk = ( S, A, P k, r ). All of them share the state space, the action space and the reward function. They differ only in their transition dynamics Pk,that is, in their physics. During training, the environments are presented one after the other, each for a fixed budget. In the skill ablation, the budget is a number of episodes; when ENA is compared with other learners, it is a number of environment steps (Section IV). The agent observes only states, actions, rewards and episode ends. It is never told when the environment changes, which environment it is in, or how many there are (Appendix A), and it keeps all of its state across the changes. After training, the agent is tested, frozen, on a sequence that contains every training environment and some it has never seen. Again, it is not told where one environment ends and the next begins. An agent forgets environment Mk to the extent that its test return there falls below its return at the end of Mk’s training phase. Section IV-D measures this as retention and absolute forgetting. An agent adapts when it learns each new environment within its budget. The goal is to adapt to every new environment without forgetting the earlier ones. This setting is narrower than continual RL in general [2]: the observation space, the action space and the reward stay the same, and only the physics change. 

B. Overview and Terms 

The Evolutionary Neuroplastic Agent (ENA) never com-putes a gradient. It maintains a population of N small neural-network policies, the specialists , and each specialist carries a scalar Trust score. In every training episode, one specialist acts, and its return updates its Trust. A Plasticity value then decides how much of the population is re-evaluated. The acting specialist may be archived in a Hall of Fame , and the population evolves. Two more skills make the archive usable when the environment changes. Environment detection 

notices when the physics change and recognizes physics seen before. Specialist selection chooses which archived specialist to deploy. Each skill is an interchangeable module with an “off” setting, which makes the ablation of Section V possible. Fig. 1 summarizes one training episode. C. Inspiration 

ENA borrows two strategies from nature. Each one works through a mechanism that evolutionary algorithms for chang-ing environments already use. 

1) Variation in proportion to stress (Plasticity): Bacteria under stress increase their mutation rate. The response is reg-ulated and transient. Variation rises when the current genotype is failing and stays low otherwise, because most mutations in a well-adapted organism are harmful or neutral [28]. Evolution-ary computation adopted the idea as triggered hypermutation ,which raises the mutation rate when performance drops in a non-stationary environment [29]. More generally, it studies the control of an algorithm’s parameters during the run [30]. ENA’s Plasticity extends this response from the mutation rate to the whole search effort. The failure signal (1) sets three things: how many specialists are re-evaluated (a random subset of size k), how long each is evaluated ( s steps), and how far mutants move from their parents. The share of the population replaced in each generation stays constant. What scales with failure is the evidence gathered before selection, and the size of the steps taken after it. The rationale is cost. Every candidate step is an environment step, so a converged agent should spend few of them, while an agent that has just met new physics needs to search more broadly. Freshly initialized specialists also enter the population in every generation, as random immigrants [31] do. Their purpose is to keep the population diverse enough to follow a moving optimum. 

2) Consolidated memory (Hall of Fame): The complemen-tary learning systems theory [32], [33] holds that the brain learns quickly in the hippocampus and consolidates slowly into the neocortex. There, knowledge is protected from interference by new learning. The theory was proposed in part to explain why the brain does not suffer the catastrophic interference of gradient-trained networks [1]. ENA follows the same division of labor. The population is the fast learner, overwritten in every generation. The Hall of Fame is the consolidated store. It is entered only through a gate, it is never modified, and evolution never replaces it. In evolutionary computation, a hall of fame first kept the best individuals of past generations in competitive coevo-lution [34]. Memory-enhanced evolutionary algorithms store good solutions and reinsert them when earlier conditions return [35]. ENA adds two things to this kind of memory. The memory is indexed by the physics in which each specialist succeeded (Section III-H). Recall is a Bayesian decision rather than a reinsertion (Section III-I). 

D. Population and Specialists 

Each specialist is a small multi-layer perceptron. On Cart-Pole, its hidden layers have 4 × 2 ReLU units, with orthogonal initialization. Its output layer is linear, and the action with the highest output is played. On HalfCheetah, the hidden layers have 32 × 16 tanh units. Tanh hidden layers follow the evolution-strategies policy of Salimans et al. [17], and normc initialization follows its released code [36]. The output is also a tanh there, as in the actor networks of CEM-RL [20], which keeps actions in their range. A specialist’s inputs are normalized with a running mean and variance. These statistics keep drifting while the agent explores. Before a specialist acts, its first layer is re-scaled analytically to the current statistics, so that its behavior is preserved without retraining. During training, the acting specialist is chosen ϵ-greedily. With probability ϵ, it is a random non-archived specialist; otherwise, it is the specialist with the highest Trust.

F. Evolutionary Search 

After every training episode, the specialists are ranked by Trust, and ties go to the older specialist. A specialist’s age counts the episodes it has played, and a mutant inherits its parent’s age. The top 20% form the elite pool. The bottom 80%, except archived specialists, are replaced. Of the replaced specialists, 40% become mutants of a random elite and 40% become crossovers of two elites. The remaining 20% are freshly initialized, which keeps the population diverse. In all, with no archived specialist among them, each generation replaces 32%, 32% and 16% of the population with mutants, crossovers and fresh specialists. A mutant perturbs each weight of its parent with probability 

pm, by Gaussian noise of standard deviation 0.01 + ( σm −

0.01) P. A crossover takes each weight from either parent with probability 0.5, after both parents are re-scaled to the current input statistics. New specialists start at the reference Trust. 

G. Hall of Fame 

The Hall of Fame stores frozen copies of proven specialists, the gladiators . It holds at most K gladiators per niche ,a niche being a set of physics recognized by environment detection (Section III-H). The evolutionary step never replaces a gladiator. After each training episode, the acting specialist is considered for induction, in five steps: 1) Gate. Its Trust must stand out from the population, z =(T − μT )/ max( σT , 0.1) ≥ 1.5 (1 − 0.5 P), or reach 

T ≥ 0.5. High Plasticity lowers the induction threshold during active search. 2) Niche. Environment detection resolves which niche the episode was played in. 3) Cloning, not relabeling. When a gladiator also succeeds in a different niche, it is cloned into that niche. The original retains its home niche assignment. 4) Diversity. A candidate may be a near-copy of a gladiator of the same niche, with a cosine similarity of weights above 0.95 . It then replaces that twin only if its Trust is higher. Weight similarity is a cheap but imperfect proxy for behavioral similarity. It catches near-copies, such as a mutant and its parent. Two networks can still behave alike with different weights, for instance with permuted hidden units. 5) Capacity. A candidate fills a free slot of its niche, or replaces the niche’s weakest gladiator if it is better. This design decouples policy preservation from ongoing adap-tation:  

> •

Retention. A gladiator is a frozen copy. The Trust stored with it never decays. It rises only when the same gladiator passes the gate again in its niche, with a higher Trust. Only a candidate of its own niche with strictly higher Trust can displace it. Gladiators of other niches can never evict it, however long its own physics stay away. Gladiators in the population are also exempt from the evolutionary replacement and from the Trust blend.  

> •

Within-niche improvement. Within a niche, the archive continues to improve. A better candidate takes a free slot or the weakest gladiator’s place, and high Plasticity lowers the gate. Success in a new niche creates a clone there, instead of moving the original. Stored Trust is relative to the return history H at the time it was computed (3). H changes as the population improves, and it is cleared at each confirmed change. Trust values stored at different times are therefore not on one scale. A gladiator stored early in a regime, against weak recent returns, can outrank a later and stronger candidate, and keep it out of a full niche. Section V-D compares this ranking with the test returns. On HalfCheetah, returns vary widely with initial conditions. Admission on a single episode then risks keeping specialists that were merely lucky. There, ENA uses an Olympic variant of the Hall of Fame. Every 200,000 environment steps, all gladiators of the current niche replay 3 full episodes on shared seeds, an Olympiad . A gladiator is evicted if its mean Olympic return, normalized like Trust, falls below the reference Trust. The others are confirmed, with that mean return. Each niche holds 2 extra seats for gladiators that have not competed yet. Only confirmed gladiators are deployed at test. Before testing, gladiators are re-injected into the popula-tion, round-robin across niches, up to a share of 0.5 of the population. The selection of Section III-I then chooses among them.

The rationale is that a wrong dynamics model can change the spread of the residuals, not only their mean, and should fit worse than the right one, not better. 

2) Identification: The recent transitions, up to 500, are matched to the niche j∗ with the highest mean per-step log-likelihood. They are recognized as j∗ if it explains them within a margin of m = 2 nats per step of a model fitted on those same transitions. Otherwise, the physics are new, and a niche is created. Only niches fitted on at least 100 transitions take part. 

3) Detect, then verify: An alarm is only a candidate. After 50 post-alarm transitions, the identification test is run against the alarmed niche. If that niche still explains them, the detector treats the alarm as coming from states that its model had not yet covered. The transitions are then added to the model, and the CuSum restarts. Otherwise, a change of physics is declared, and the post-alarm transitions seed the new regime’s evidence. During the test, the dynamics models are never updated. The detector still detects and identifies, but it learns nothing new. 

4) Late identity binding: A niche is assigned only when a specialist is considered for the Hall of Fame (step 2 above). The assignment uses the transitions of the episode the spe-cialist just played. The resolved niche’s model is refined with them, or a new niche is fitted on them. 

5) Chirp detection (HalfCheetah): On HalfCheetah, the transition models misfire. On the detection benchmark of Section V-F, they confirm many more changes, and create many more niches, than there are environments. This happens even when a single fixed gait is played throughout. It happens more often still when the gait changes, as it does in a learning population (another gait, a flip, standing still). The cause was not isolated. Two plausible ones are that a linear model fits the contact-rich dynamics of a running body poorly, and that its evidence is the acting specialist’s own transitions. On HalfCheetah, ENA plays a chirp instead: a fixed probing sequence of actions, as in system identification with a known input signal [40]. At every episode end, ENA resets the envi-ronment and plays the same 64-step action sequence, whatever any specialist would do. It then compares the observations this produces across niches. Probing an environment to infer its dynamics has also been learned in RL [41]. ENA’s chirp is fixed, so its response cannot depend on the agent. 

What is played. The excitation is a multiresolution wavelet train. The Mexican hat wavelet is played at dyadic scales, from a segment of half the chirp down to the finest. The aim is to excite both the slow and the fast responses of the body. 

What is measured. The response is the observations minus the chirp’s starting observation. Each observation channel is decomposed into a Haar wavelet packet tree of depth 3 [42]. Like the discrete wavelet transform [43], the tree is dyadic and non-overlapping, but it splits every band. This gives 8 frequency bands of equal width, and one log-energy is kept per band. In machine fault diagnosis, the energies of wavelet-packet bands are widely used to characterize faults [44]. The transforms are computed with PyWavelets [45]. The excitation and the features were chosen by the ablation of Section V-F, in the three HalfCheetah training environments. ENA’s other HalfCheetah settings were chosen on tuning seeds disjoint from the study’s replicates.

At test, the belief starts uniform when the test begins. It is reset to uniform only when the detector confirms a change of physics. As in training, the test environments follow one another unannounced. Three quantities still adapt at test: the belief, the selection’s performance model, and the detector’s alarms. The weights, Plasticity ( P = 0 ) and the detector’s models stay fixed. A confirmed change still resets the input statistics. B. Protocols 1) Skill ablation (CartPole): Each ENA variant (Sec-tion IV-C1, Table IV) trains for 500 episodes per environment. It is then tested for 200 episodes per environment. The budget is a number of episodes, not of environment steps. The variants with Plasticity evaluate many more candidates, so they also interact far more with the environment (Section V-A). A total of 30 independent replicates were run. Replicate 

k (k = 1 , 2, . . . ) seeds Python’s, NumPy’s and TensorFlow’s random number generators with 1000 + k − 1 before each agent is built, trained and tested. Every environment is seeded with numbers derived from the same seed. All the agents of a replicate therefore start from the same random state and meet the same initial states: they differ only in what they are. Every study in this paper seeds its replicates the same way. 

2) Forgetting in single-policy learners: To measure how much standard learners forget on these streams, single-policy learners train on the same streams as ENA. Comparing their forgetting with ENA’s is only fair at equal interaction. Every agent therefore trains on each environment for the same budget of environment steps: 2,500,000 on CartPole and 1,000,000 on HalfCheetah. ENA’s budget includes every step it spends evaluating candidates (4), holding Olympiads and playing its chirp (Section III-H5), not only the episodes of its acting specialist. Each agent stops at the end of the episode in which it reaches the budget. ENA’s episode includes these extra steps, so ENA can exceed the budget slightly. ENA spends 84.9% of its CartPole training steps evaluating candidates. It therefore plays far fewer episodes within the same budget: about 815 per environment on CartPole, against 6,759 for DQN, 5,791 for PPO and 5,927 for SAC-Discrete. On HalfCheetah, candidate evaluation, Olympiads and chirps take most of its steps. ENA plays about 214 episodes per environment there, against 1,000 for PPO and 1,000 for SAC. Training is compared against environment steps, not episodes, so that every agent spans the same budget. The frozen test needs no such normalization, since every agent plays the same number of test episodes. No agent observes the physical parameters, or is told when the environment changes. Appendix A lists what each agent receives. On CartPole, the agent is evaluated, frozen, every 5,000 training steps; ENA is evaluated through its highest-Trust spe-cialist. Each check plays 5 episodes on a separate copy of the current environment. The checks never stop or alter training, and their steps do not count as training. They record the first solve : the number of training steps into an environment at which the evaluation mean first reaches the success threshold. A replicate that never solves an environment is counted at the end of its phase. HalfCheetah uses no intermediate checks. First solve is not measured there, since ENA never reaches its threshold and PPO almost never does. After training, all agents are tested frozen. On CartPole they play 200 episodes per environment, as in the ablation, and on HalfCheetah 50. The CartPole comparison runs 50 replicates. Its 3 comparisons, ENA-Combined against each baseline on test reliability, form one Holm family. This family was planned before the runs. Since the single-policy learners serve only as a reference, the result is read as the overall cost of their forgetting, not as a ranking of the agents. The HalfCheetah comparison runs 20 replicates. Its two comparisons, ENA-Combined against PPO and against SAC on left-behind re-tention, form one Holm family.

The non-stationary stream made two changes necessary, both found in full-length runs before the reported study:  

> •

PPO uses a constant learning rate. With the usual linear annealing to zero over the training budget, PPO reaches its last environment with almost no learning rate left. A learner in a non-stationary stream has no reason to learn less in a later environment than in an earlier one.  

> •

SAC-Discrete uses a target entropy of 0.5 log |A| , instead of 0.98 log |A| . With CartPole’s two actions, 0.98 log 2 

is nearly the maximum entropy. The policy cannot reach it, and the temperature keeps growing. A ratio of 0.89 diverged in the same way, with a temperature of 4,907 and values of 40,174. At 0.5, the temperature peaked at 3.7 and the values at 121. During the frozen test, the baselines act deterministically with their final parameters, SAC with the mean of its policy. Section III-I describes what still adapts in ENA at test. 

D. Metrics  

> •

Reliability (CartPole): the percentage of test episodes whose return reaches the success threshold, over all five test environments. The tables also give it over the three training environments alone, which leaves out the unseen ones.  

> •

Retention : the test return as a percentage of the end-of-phase training return. It is computed for each training environment, and averaged over the environments left behind, that is, all training environments except the last. It is a ratio per replicate, so its mean can differ from the ratio of the mean returns. The end-of-phase return is the mean return of the training episodes that end in the last tenth of the phase: of its step budget in the comparisons, and of its episodes in the ablation. Retention is the primary metric on HalfCheetah, and it is reported descriptively on CartPole. End-of-phase returns include every agent’s training exploration, so retention can exceed 100%. For an environment the agent never learned, retention measures nothing.  

> •

Absolute forgetting : the end-of-phase training return mi-nus the test return, in return units. It is computed for each training environment, and averaged over the environments left behind. Unlike retention, it does not reward an agent for having learned little. Its returns tell a weak policy kept intact from a strong policy partly lost.  

> •

Mean return per environment.  

> •

Steps to first solve per training environment (CartPole comparison only), at the 5,000-step resolution of the evaluation checks.  

> •

Compute : training environment steps, and ENA’s candidate-evaluation steps among them, which are prop-erties of the algorithms; and wall-clock training time, which also depends on the implementations and hardware (Appendix C). Every metric is computed per replicate, and reported as the mean and standard deviation over replicates. All the agents of a replicate share its seed (Section IV-B), so every comparison is paired by replicate. Each difference between two agents is reported in three ways: the mean of the within-replicate differences, with its 95% confidence interval; the effect size 

dz , which is that mean over the standard deviation of the differences; and a paired t-test at α = 0 .05 . Steps to first solve are the exception. A few slow replicates dominate their mean, so they are reported as the median and interquartile range, and compared with the two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test. The skill ablation makes 7 planned comparisons: each skill is added on its own, and on top of the others. They are treated as one family, and their p-values are corrected with Holm’s method. The number of replicates was fixed before the runs, by a power analysis for two independent groups. With 30 replicates per variant, a two-sided test at the family’s most stringent level ( 0.05 /7) has 80% power for effects of d ≥ 0.94 .Here d is the difference of means over the root mean square of the two standard deviations. Analyzed paired, the family has 80% power for dz ≥ 0.69 . Welch’s unpaired t-test reaches the same decision in 20 of the 20 Holm-corrected comparisons of this paper. The selection study forms its own Holm family (Sec-tion V-D). The Plasticity-controller study forms one family per metric (Section V-C). The HalfCheetah comparison on absolute forgetting, ENA-Combined against PPO and SAC, forms another family, defined after the runs. Retention and absolute forgetting on CartPole, in the ablation and in the comparison, are descriptive and uncorrected, and so is every per-environment comparison.

C. Shape of the Plasticity Controller 

The linear ramp controller (1) was compared with the two alternatives from Section III-E3: the quadratic and the fuzzy controller. Each alternative replaced only ENA-Combined’s Plasticity controller, and ran for 30 replicates with the ab-lation’s seeds. Every agent of a replicate starts from the same random state. The ablation’s ENA-Combined is therefore the linear-ramp run of the same replicates, and serves as the reference without being re-run. The decision rule was fixed before the runs. An alternative replaces the ramp if its test reliability is significantly higher, or if it is not significantly lower and uses significantly fewer candidate-evaluation steps. For each metric, the two compar-isons with the ramp form one Holm family. At 30 replicates, each family has 80% power for dz ≥ 0.59 . Table IX gives the comparisons. The ramp achieved a test reliability of 83.2% (16.1), with 4,226,921 candidate steps per replicate. The fuzzy controller achieved 83.9% (11.4), which is not distinguishable from the ramp’s, and it spent significantly more candidate steps. The quadratic controller achieved 89.3% (7.7) with fewer candidate steps, but neither difference is significant. Its effect on relia-bility ( dz = 0 .35 ) is below the detectable one. Resolving it with 80% power would take about 79 replicates per controller. The rule’s second clause reads a loss that is not significant as no loss, which is not evidence of equivalence. It did not decide this outcome, since neither alternative used significantly fewer candidate steps. A confirmatory test of a cheaper controller would need a non-inferiority margin fixed in advance. For the quadratic controller, the 95% interval of the reliability difference runs from −0.3 to 12.4 points. By this criterion, the controller retained is the linear ramp. As an exploratory observation outside the planned compar-isons, the quadratic controller’s reliability varied less across replicates, with a standard deviation of 7.7 against 16.1.

RQ1a: is an archive enough by itself? No. Variants with a Hall of Fame but no detection are not more reliable than the same variants without it, and ENA-HoF is less reliable than ENA-NoSkill. Variants whose archive is organized by detected physics are more reliable than those with an archive but no detection, in both cases. With Plasticity, they are also more reliable than the variant without any archive, and only this combination maintains performance across all learned environments (Section V-A). In this ablation, merely archiv-ing proven specialists proved insufficient: archiving increased reliability only together with detection. Detection, however, brings three things at once: the niche index, a larger archive, and resets at each confirmed change (Section IV-C1). Which of them matters was not isolated. The recall rule also matters. Bayesian policy reuse is more reliable than deploying the recognized niche’s highest-Trust gladiator. Niche match, in turn, is not significantly more reliable than ignoring the niche (Section V-D). With either simpler rule, the archive is not more reliable than no archive at all. 

RQ1b: does the memory cost extra interaction? Partly answered. Without Plasticity, archiving and detecting add no measurable candidate evaluation of their own. Their only mea-sured cost is wall-clock time (Section V-A). With Plasticity, adding the Hall of Fame comes with more candidate steps. The increase is not significant, but the data cannot exclude it, up to 822,475 steps per replicate. The archive takes no extra candidate evaluation to fill, but it may change how much the failure-driven search spends. 

RQ1c: can the memory be built online, without labels? Yes in both domains, with different drivers. On CartPole, the transition-model detector separates the training environments, with the fragmentation and false alarms of Table XVI. On HalfCheetah, the same kind of detector fails (Section III-H5). The chirp, whose response does not depend on the agent, meets the detection target on the benchmark. It also gives each training environment its own niche in every replicate of the HalfCheetah study (Section V-F). Neither driver uses a gradient. 

RQ2: does failure-driven search pay? Partly supported. The comparisons that add Plasticity show the largest differences in the ablation, and only the variants with Plasticity learn the harder environments (Section V-A). In the ablation, however, the budget is a number of episodes, so more search also means far more interaction with the environment. Switching Plasticity off also changes the candidate fitness signal, not only its volume. At the minimum depth, a CartPole candidate that survives smin steps is credited with a full episode (Sec-tion III-E). The ablation does not separate the two. Searching at full effort in every episode costs an order of magnitude more candidate evaluation (Section V-B), a cost set by construction. Whether the ramp reaches what full effort does remains open. In the single replicate run at full effort, full effort was the more reliable. Whether scaling search effort dynamically with failure outperforms distributing identical effort uniformly across iterations remains an open empirical question. The quadratic controller was more reliable than the ramp and used fewer candidate steps, but neither difference is significant (Section V-C). These findings therefore support the general principle of scaling search with failure, rather than the specific linear ramp evaluated here. 

B. How Detection May Help the Archive 

Detection may help the archive in three ways, and the ablation does not separate them.  

> •

The index at recall. Without niches, the archive is one pool of gladiators proven in different physics. At test time, the selection mechanism then lacks dynamical evi-dence to distinguish specialists evolved for Jupiter from those evolved for Earth. With detection, every gladiator carries the physics it was proven in. The dynamics likelihoods of the selection (8) point it to the matching niche, and the gladiators of one niche can no longer be evicted by another niche’s.  

> •

The capacity. With detection, the archive holds K glad-iators per niche instead of K in all, so it ends training several times larger (Section V-A). During training, its protected copies also hold more population slots, what-ever the current physics.  

> •

The resets. Each confirmed change clears the return history and the input statistics, and returns the failure signal to its maximum for the next episodes. Without detection, none of this happens. An archive of matched capacity, with the same resets but with-out the niche index, would separate the index from the other two. Adding the archive also replaces a random population member by Bayesian policy reuse (Section IV-C1). Selection rules that use the same niches also differ in reliability. Bayesian policy reuse differs from niche match in two ways. It weighs each episode’s dynamics likelihoods into a belief, instead of following the detector’s single decision. It also learns from the test returns. The selection ablation does not separate the two. One plausible mechanism, not directly isolated in the experiments, involves the feedback provided by episode returns. They let Bayesian policy reuse abandon a gladiator that fails in its own niche, which niche match cannot do, and across a test phase, niche match evaluates substantially fewer distinct individuals (Section V-D). A glad-iator archived for solving a niche could still fail there at test, and without return-based evidence, an ineffective specialist would continue to be deployed. Niche match also inherits the archive’s ranking. It plays a niche’s gladiator of highest stored Trust, and Trust values stored at different times are not on one scale (Section III-G). In the test phases where Bayesian policy reuse settled on another gladiator of the same niche, it was mostly the more reliable (Section V-D). Part of its advantage may therefore come from correcting that ranking with the test returns. The experiments do not separate this from its use of the dynamics evidence. 

C. Retention, Adaptation and Absolute Return 

ENA preserves the behaviors it acquires. Other learners, however, adapt faster or reach higher returns, by margins that vary with the domain. Both gaps match known limits of evolutionary search. Evolutionary algorithms need many samples [19], [20], and struggle on problems that require optimizing many parameters [19]. The cost of exploring in parameter space grows with the number of parameters [49], and random mutations tend to disrupt large networks [50]. This is why ENA’s specialists are small. On CartPole, PPO first solves every training environment sooner than ENA. Most of ENA’s steps go to evaluating candidates (Section IV-B2). Against DQN and SAC-Discrete, the order depends on the environment: ENA solves Mars sooner than DQN, and Earth later than both (Section V-E). SAC-Discrete scores slightly higher on the environments it does not forget. ENA, in turn, preserves Jupiter, where every single-policy learner degrades. On HalfCheetah, the gap is larger. ENA preserves perfor-mance from earlier phases, but achieves lower asymptotic returns than SAC within the allocated step budget. The robot has six joints to control. PPO and SAC control them with hidden layers of 256 × 256 ReLU units, while ENA’s spe-cialists have 32 × 16 tanh units. Evolving networks as large as the baselines’ would mean searching far more parameters, at the cost described above. Even with small networks, most of ENA’s steps go to evaluating candidates rather than to its acting specialist’s episodes (Section IV-B2). What SAC loses on the environments left behind exceeds the return ENA reached there at the end of each phase (Section V-G). Most of ENA’s retention above 100% comes from its end-of-phase reference, which includes episodes of specialists outside the archive. Measured against its gladiators’ episodes alone, its retention is 103.2% (Section V-G). A specialist archive is hypothesized to provide the greatest advantage when environments recur and relearning is com-putationally expensive, though this setting was not explicitly evaluated. When an environment is unlikely to return, or a gradient learner can relearn it quickly, a single strong policy that is partly forgotten may be worth more than a weaker one kept intact. 

D. Scope of the Comparison 

The single-policy learners measure how much standard learners forget on these streams. The claim of this paper rests on the skill ablation. The comparison has three limits. First, at test, every agent’s weights are frozen, but ENA still routes among its archived specialists with an updating belief. The single-policy learners deploy one policy. The comparison thus contrasts context-indexed specialist preservation with single-policy learning, not two equally equipped systems. Second, ENA evaluates its candidates in fresh copies of the current environment, which the experiment builds from the environment’s own parameters (Appendix A). Every candidate step counts in ENA’s budget. Still, the single-policy learners play only in the stream itself, and never use such a copy. Third, the continual-RL methods closest to ENA are not baselines here, for reasons that differ between them. OWL [5] and TeLAPA [24] are told where tasks begin and end. OWL trains the head of the task it is given, and infers the task only at test. TeLAPA builds a task’s archive, and retrieves a starting point from its archives, at each task boundary it is given. Both build their memory during the stream, but from labeled tasks. On the non-stationary streams evaluated here, these methods would require either explicit task boundaries—which are unavailable to any agent in this study—or an environment detector to infer them. Providing such an inferential mech-anism would effectively endow those baselines with ENA’s environment-detection capability. ITE [23] builds its archive offline, before deployment. It adapts once to each damage, starting when performance drops below a user-set threshold. It neither keeps a memory across a sequence of conditions, nor recognizes a condition that returns. RL-CD [6], MBCD [7] and SWOKS [8] do detect contexts online without labels, and keep a policy per context, so they address the same problem. A comparison with them, however, would be confounded. RL-CD’s models are tabular and need a discretized state space, while MBCD and SWOKS learn by gradient descent. A difference could therefore come from the learning algorithm rather than from the memory. Direct comparisons with such algorithms are deferred to future investigations of hybrid architectures combining ENA’s modular skills with gradient-based optimization (Section VII). One promising formulation would train specialist policies per niche via gradient descent while retaining ENA’s environment detection and Bayesian policy reuse. Finally, wall-clock execution times reflect specific soft-ware implementations, measured on a single workstation for CartPole and on dedicated cloud instances per replicate for HalfCheetah. While environment steps and candidate steps represent intrinsic properties of the algorithms, wall-clock time also reflects implementation and hardware utilization efficiency; hence, it does not imply that ENA is inherently less computationally demanding across all execution platforms. 

E. Limitations  

> •

Two benchmark families. Both use related physics changes, with a single morphology in each. Visual, partially observable or structurally different tasks are untested.  

> •

Fixed task order. Each domain uses one training se-quence (Mars–Jupiter–Earth; Earth–Heavy-Rough–Mars-Slippery). Ordering effects are untested.  

> •

No recurrence during training. Earlier environments re-turn only at the frozen test. Repeated switches, short dwell times and recall during training are untested.  

> •

Test-time choice in the ablation. Without a Hall of Fame, there are no gladiators to choose from. In that case, ENA-NoSkill and ENA-Plasticity play a random member of their final population in every test episode. The variants with an archive choose among gladiators by Bayesian policy reuse. Adding the Hall of Fame thus changes both what is kept and how the test policy is chosen, and the ablation does not separate the two.  

> •

Detection in the ablation. Switching detection on also gives the archive K gladiators per niche instead of K in all. It also resets the return history and the input statistics at each confirmed change. The ablation does not separate these from the niche index (Section IV-C1).  

> •

Interaction in the ablation. The ablation fixes the number of episodes, not of environment steps. A variant with Plasticity takes many times more steps than one without (Section V-A). Without Plasticity, every candidate is also evaluated for only smin steps, which changes its fitness signal. The ablation shows what failure-driven search achieves with that interaction. It does not show that allocating effort by failure beats spreading the same effort evenly. Full search was run in a single replicate.  

> •

Resets. The chirp requires resetting the environment at episode boundaries, to play its 64-step excitation from nominal initial states. Candidate evaluation needs more than a reset: a resettable copy of the current physics. Both are natural in simulation, digital twins and automated test rigs. They constrain continuous, non-resettable physical systems. VII. C ONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 

This work investigated whether a small set of modular skills can transform the solutions generated by an evolving population into a reusable memory that mitigates catastrophic forgetting. To address this question, the Evolutionary Neu-roplastic Agent (ENA) was introduced. Across the evaluated CartPole sequences, the empirical ablation supports this ar-chitecture. Only variants with failure-driven search master the more demanding environments, requiring increased environ-ment interaction. Among these variants, only the architec-ture that indexes its archive by detected physical regimes preserves acquired competencies across switches. Introducing the archive also alters test-time policy selection, an effect that the current ablation does not decouple. Detection also enlarges the archive and resets the agent’s statistics at each change, and the ablation does not separate these from the index either. Policy retrieval via Bayesian policy reuse proved significantly more reliable than relying solely on change detection. The memory is constructed online without ex-ternal supervision, using detection mechanisms tailored to each domain. Whether dynamically modulating search effort based on failure outperforms uniform effort allocation remains an open question. Single-policy learners trained on identical sequences suffer catastrophic forgetting of previously acquired behaviors. Conversely, they highlight regimes where ENA is less competitive: on CartPole, adaptation is slower than PPO, and on HalfCheetah, asymptotic returns are lower than those of SAC. Both limitations align with known properties of evolutionary search, which exhibits high sample complexity and scales unfavorably with parameter count [19], [49]. The most direct next step is a hybrid of ENA with gradient-based learners. Evolution and gradient descent can be com-bined in multiple configurations, offering several promising avenues. Gradient learners such as SAC or PPO could serve as local optimizers within each niche to accelerate adaptation and achieve higher asymptotic returns. ENA’s environment detection, Plasticity, and physics-indexed Hall of Fame would preserve niche-specific competencies across dynamic switches. Alternatively, the architectures could partition the policy network—with gradient descent optimizing high-dimensional feature representations and evolutionary search tuning low-dimensional control layers—or alternate sequentially. The evolutionary population could also supply diverse rollouts to off-policy replay buffers, as in evolutionary reinforcement learning [19]. A hybrid architecture would furthermore pro-vide a direct comparison against continual-RL frameworks designed for contextual non-stationarity: OWL [5], RL-CD [6], MBCD [7], SWOKS [8], and TeLAPA [24]. The modular skills may also transfer directly to those frame-works. OWL and TeLAPA depend on explicit task boundaries. Their authors list detecting boundaries during training [5] and weaker task boundaries [24] among future directions. ITE ini-tiates adaptation based on a predefined performance threshold and maintains no memory across sequential conditions [23]. ENA’s environment detection identifies transitions and recog-nizes recurring regimes directly from unassisted observations, providing capabilities that existing methods either assume or lack. Furthermore, a physics-indexed archive could preserve adapted policies for deployment when previously encountered conditions recur. Several further directions remain open. Repeated recurrence during training and randomized task orders would test the memory beyond the evidence here. For systems that cannot be reset, the probe could be superimposed on the agent’s own actions as a small dither. An archive of matched capacity without the niche index would isolate what the index con-tributes. Evaluating candidates for a fixed horizon, whatever the Plasticity, would separate the volume of search from the fidelity of its fitness signal. The archive could also rank its gladiators by a stationary score, such as their own-niche returns on shared seeds, as the Olympiads do on HalfCheetah. The contributions of the belief’s two sources of evidence, the dynamics likelihoods and the test returns, remain to be separated. Specialist selection could also be learned, as a contextual bandit over the detector’s evidence. Finally, the population’s evaluations are independent and parallelize nat-urally on accelerators, which would allow larger populations and longer non-stationary streams. AUTHOR CONTRIBUTIONS

APPENDIX AINFORMATION AVAILABLE TO EACH AGENT 

A comparison across agent families is only fair if no agent learns more about the environment than the others. This appendix lists everything each agent receives. 

A. The interface 

Every agent, ENA and the baselines alike, interacts with an environment only through the Gymnasium interface. A reset returns an initial observation. A step returns the next observation, the reward, and whether the episode terminated or was truncated by the time limit. In CartPole, the observation is the cart’s position and velocity, and the pole’s angle and angular velocity. In HalfCheetah, it is the torso’s height and angle, the joint angles, and all their velocities, without the forward position. The physical parameters that distinguish the environments are never part of the observation. In CartPole, these are gravity, the pole’s mass and the pole’s length. In HalfCheetah, they are gravity, friction, wind and the presence of air. No agent is told when the environment changes, which environment it is in, or how many environments there are. The environment names in the figures and tables are taken from the experiment logs, and are used only for analysis. ENA’s niches are its own numbering. 

B. Task constants 

ENA reads two constants from each task’s Gymnasium registration: the episode time limit, and the solved threshold 

Rsolved . They are 500 steps and 475 in CartPole, and 1,000 steps and 4,800 in HalfCheetah. The time limit rescales a truncated candidate return to a full-episode equivalent (4). HalfCheetah episodes never end early, and its candidates are evaluated for at most 200 steps, so every HalfCheetah can-didate’s return is rescaled. The solved threshold is the utility scale of the specialist selection (8). The selection therefore depends on this registered constant, even on HalfCheetah, where no agent reaches it. There, the threshold sets how much one return moves the belief, since the return noise of (8) is 0.2 times the threshold in return units. Until 10 returns of the current regime have been seen, it is also the scale against which returns are normalized for Trust. After that, Trust normalizes against the percentiles of recent returns (3). Both constants are public properties of the task, not of the physics. The baselines meet the same time limit through the truncation signal. 

C. Environment detection 

The detector’s input is the sequence of transitions 

(st, a t, s t+1 ): the observation before acting, the action taken, and the observation after. This is exactly what the agent sees. The detector does not read the reward, the physical parameters or the environment’s name. A change of physics changes how the observed velocities evolve, so the current niche’s dynamics model (5) predicts the new transitions worse. In the ablation, every switch between training environments is confirmed in every replicate, within a few episodes. False alarms also occur, mostly in the first training phase (Table XVI). At test, the detector is frozen and creates no niches. Physics close to a known niche are recognized as that niche. The detector does not need to name an environment, only to find the closest physics it knows. Neptune’s physics, for instance, are recognized as Earth’s in 30 of the 30 replicates. When no niche explains the transitions within the margin m, the episode is left unrecognized, and no niche is assigned. The selection (8) still weighs every niche by how well its dynamics model explains the episode, so it falls back on the niches whose models explain the episode best. > •

False alarms. In a test phase, every confirmed change except the first one after a switch is a false alarm. In physics near the margin m, the detector can alternate between recognizing a niche and leaving the physics unrecognized. Each confirmation resets the selection’s belief to uniform.  

> •

Fragmentation. In some replicates, one training environ-ment is split over two niches. Table XVI gives these numbers for the two ablation variants with environment detection. The first training phase, Mars, starts with no niche at all. The detector is also reset when the test begins, so the test’s Mars phase starts without a current niche, like the run’s first. The Brown-dwarf phase is mostly left unrecognized. After it, the detector recognizes Earth by identification, without confirming a change, and Earth shows zero confirmed changes at test. D. Candidate evaluation 

ENA evaluates candidate specialists in fresh instances of the current environment. The experiment’s own environment factory builds them from the current environment’s physical parameters. ENA passes these parameters to the factory, but uses none of them for a decision. Every candidate step counts in ENA’s interaction budget. A candidate evaluation is thus equivalent to an extra episode in the current environment, with the same reset that any episodic agent has. The baselines could spend the same budget in parallel copies of the environment, as vectorized training does. What ENA does assume is a resettable copy of the current physics, in which it can run episodes other than the one being played. Such a copy is free in simulation or with a digital twin. On a physical system, it would cost real time, if it can be had at all. 

E. Evaluation and test 

The first-solve checks (Section IV-B2) run on a separate copy of the current environment, for every agent alike. Their steps do not count as training. Fig. 5 shows the frozen test of the CartPole comparison. Each agent plays 200 episodes in each of five environments: Mars-Light → Neptune → Jupiter-Heavy → Brown-dwarf
