Abstract
Reasoning language models struggle with real-time control when the time spent gen-
erating a decision exceeds the available response window. System-One Recursive Self-
Improvement (System-One RSI) converts task knowledge and interaction experience into
reusable visual judgments. A team of agents built on reasoning models supplies control rules
and supervision through informal language, guiding targeted practice in the environment.
The decision model stores learned distinctions in its parameters and returns structured
judgments without generating a reasoning trace at every step. An Observe, Orient, Decide,
Act (OODA) controller uses these judgments to select actions. Recorded experience supports
subsequent model training and controller revision without requiring expert operational
demonstrations. Deployments in Zenless Zone Zero and Black Myth: Wukong examine
prediction accuracy and online behaviour. The studies include visual-model adaptation and
controller revision across opponents. The framework targets environments where repeated
interaction is costly and parallel simulation is impractical. Its application depends on task
knowledge, the coverage of collected experience, and the resources available for inference.

Introduction
Long-chain reasoning has improved large language models’ ability to solve complex text-
processing tasks by allowing them to develop and revise intermediate steps before producing
an answer [27]. Paired with multimodal perception and action interfaces, the same mechanism
supports computer use and high-level planning for robot control [1, 2]. The competence is
general; the timing is not. Greater reasoning effort requires more intermediate tokens, and every
additional token is another sequential decoding step [27]. Model depth adds a cost of its own,
since at comparable width and numerical precision more layers raise per-token computation and
the memory held for parameters and cached attention states [28]. In an environment that keeps
changing, the resulting delay can leave an observation stale or let an action opportunity pass
before the reasoning that identifies it has finished [29].
Decision models have recently attracted renewed interest as a means of reducing decision
latency [13,30]. Models such as JEV retain backbones similar to those of conventional large
language models but differ primarily in their output interface: a decision head or selected
output logits directly assign scores or probabilities to candidate answers without autoregressively
generating a textual response [28,30]. With relatively few layers and a single forward pass per
decision, compact models achieve reported latencies of approximately 50–200 ms on bounded
inputs, supporting a broad range of real-time applications [28]. However, without explicit
multistep reasoning, these models cannot decompose and revise decisions through intermediate
reasoning steps, making complex judgments more difficult. Careful task-specific prompt tuning
can yield high accuracy, but long prompts or multiple input images can increase processing time
severalfold, bringing latency closer to that of conventional language models [28,30]. Moreover,
prompt tuning and few-shot examples cannot readily convey fine-grained multimodal experience.
For example, precise dodge timing requires distinguishing the visually similar postures in
Fig. 1(a)–(c) and initiating a dodge only at frame (c). Reliable decisions in such settings require
updating the decision model’s weights to consolidate experience acquired through practice and
interaction and apply it to subsequent judgments.

Classical reinforcement learning offers a third route: it addresses acquisition rather than
expression, and its execution side is already fast. A policy implemented as a shallow network
maps an observation to an action in a short forward pass, without a reasoning trace, which suits
high-frequency control. The cost sits in obtaining the policy, which normally requires substantial
training data [10,11]. Online RL gathers that data by interacting and updating from observed
rewards, but a live environment may not be sandboxable, reliably reset, or replicable for parallel
rollouts. Account authentication and persistent service state can block each of these operations,
which makes large-scale trial and error impractical [11]. Offline RL avoids further interaction by
learning from existing trajectories, at the price of collecting them and of remaining within their
coverage, particularly for actions whose consequences the dataset represents poorly [12]. Under

No single route in Table 1 supplies all three ingredients at once: task knowledge, fast
execution, and experience from limited interaction. A reasoning model supplies the first
and, given multimodal input, reads scenes that a fixed rule set cannot anticipate. A decision
model supplies the second. Combining them suggests a natural question: can a recursive
self-improvement (RSI) procedure let the reasoning model improve the decision model from
their shared experience, so that the capability of the former becomes resident in the latter instead
of being consulted beside it?
Motor learning exhibits the same combination in an observable form. Knowledge of the
physical principles that stabilize a bicycle does not confer the ability to ride one. Those principles
do not supply the postural corrections that maintain balance, and the corrections are made too
rapidly to be governed by deliberation; a swimmer does not deliberate over each adjustment of
the stroke. Progressive practice over hours or days is generally required before the responses can
be produced without conscious reconstruction, a condition commonly termed muscle memory.
Conventional online reinforcement learning requires on the order of thousands of trials to
acquire a policy, whereas human acquisition of a comparable movement requires far fewer
attempts; the difference is the coach. A coach neither performs the movement nor describes it
completely; it watches, locates the error, and sets an exercise that isolates the error, so that practice
is spent on the missing distinction rather than on repetitions of what is already reliable [3].
This paper places a reasoning model in the coach’s role. The model names the observations
that matter, states what should be practiced, and defines how the record of an attempt should be
read; as records accumulate it revises those instructions. Supervised training consolidates the
resulting judgments in a decision model, where they are applied in a single forward pass rather
than regenerated at each step. This capacity is termed external muscle memory by analogy, and

This paper presents System-One Recursive Self-Improvement (System-One RSI). It couples
a fast inner loop with an offline outer loop (Fig. 3). The fast decision loop (inner loop) is an
Observe, Orient, Decide, Act (OODA) cycle organized around the decision model, which carries
the entire perceptual burden of the cycle: an observation enters, the decision model returns
one structured judgment per task-relevant question, a decision tree determines the action those
judgments jointly imply, and the execution interface acts before the next observation arrives.
The RSI loop (outer loop) is driven by a team of agents built on reasoning models, with roles
shown in Fig. 4. These agents act at three stages of each round. They write the decision tree
and the prompting used inside the inner loop, so the questions asked of the decision model and
the way its answers become actions are both under their control. They then collect samples,
starting from a simple inner-loop policy or even a random one, so that the first records reflect the
environment rather than a pre-existing policy. Finally they review those records offline, identify
where the inner loop failed, and choose between tuning the decision model and revising the
decision tree before the next round. Because either choice changes which states the system visits
next, the following round is built on evidence that the previous round’s system produced.
We conducted experimental validation under a variety of environmental conditions, including
three dynamic visual environments, the Snake game, Zenless Zone Zero, and Black Myth:
Wukong, with the same 27B decision model used throughout. The Snake game involves real
observation latency and multiple obstacle types; the system receives image input only and
reads no internal game state, and adaptation raises the mean score from 1.30 to 7.35. The
other two environments are game clients without internal modification, external intervention, or
sandboxing; they expose no internal state and support neither reset nor parallel instantiation. In
a typical scenario of Zenless Zone Zero, adaptation promotes action precision from 63.85% to
81.15%, raises the success rate from 25% to 100%, and reduces median completion time from
261.2 s to 239.3 s. In a combat scenario of Black Myth: Wukong, the evasive policy extends
player mean survival time from 23.6 s to 260.5 s, and the offensive policy win rate rises from 0%
to 66.7%.

System-One RSI
This paper proposes System-One RSI, which comprises a high-frequency online inner loop and
an offline outer loop for recursive improvement (Fig. 3).
The target setting differs from common simulator-based reinforcement learning settings in
three respects. (1) The environment evolves continuously in real time, even when no action is
submitted, and does not pause during decision-model inference; actions therefore take effect in
an environment that has continued to evolve, with a latency of approximately 200 ms or more
in the target deployment. (2) The environment cannot be parallelized or sandboxed, so each
episode consumes real-world time and only a very limited number of episodes can be collected.
(3) Interaction follows the human control interface: each action specifies not only when to act,
but also which key to press and how long to hold it, measured in milliseconds.

System-One RSI couples a fast inner loop with an offline outer loop (Fig. 3). The fast
decision loop (inner loop) is an Observe, Orient, Decide, Act (OODA) cycle organized around
the decision model, which carries the entire perceptual burden of the cycle: an observation
enters, the decision model returns one structured judgment per task-relevant question, a decision
tree determines the action those judgments jointly imply, and the execution interface acts before
the next observation arrives. The reasoning model takes no part in the cycle. Interpretation of
the scene is confined to a single forward pass, which is what allows the loop to close within the
response window.
The RSI loop (outer loop) encloses the inner loop and is carried out by a team of agents
built on reasoning models, with roles shown in Fig. 4. These agents collaborate at three stages
of each round. They write the decision tree and the prompting used inside the inner loop, so
the questions asked of the decision model and the way its answers become actions are both
under their control. They then collect samples, starting from a simple inner-loop policy or
even a random one, so that the first records reflect the environment rather than a pre-existing
policy. Finally, they review those records offline, identify where the inner loop failed, and choose
between tuning the decision model and revising the decision tree before the next round. Because

2.1
Coordinating the adaptation workflow
The agent team comprises a Planning & Coordination Agent, Rollout / Capture Agents, a Visual
Labeling Agent, and a Training / Deploying Agent (Fig. 4). The Planning & Coordination Agent
maintains the task specification and organizes tasks and feedback across the controller-revision
and model-adaptation paths. The Rollout / Capture Agents execute OODA and record interactions,
the Visual Labeling Agent assigns supervision labels, and the Training / Deploying Agent trains
and deploys updated model weights. Shared interaction records and deployment artifacts connect
these activities. This organization supports the two update paths while preserving an explicit
interface between judgments and actions. The coordinator connects rule development to data
collection and training, so each update returns to the same execution interface.

2.2
Reasoning model and control interface
The reasoning model turns task knowledge into control rules and a supervision specification
for recorded observations. Language-model-generated control programs have direct precedents
in Code as Policies and Voyager [7,8]. During online interaction, the controller obtains visual
observations, calls the decision model for structured judgments, applies decision rules, and
submits actions through the environment’s control interface. Task knowledge is represented in
both executable control rules and learned decision parameters.

From prior knowledge to task supervision
The framework can draw on web materials, informal human hints, reusable instructions or
programs, and a small number of exploratory interactions with the live environment. Exploratory
traces provide observations of environment states and input effects for subsequent analysis. A
reasoning model can use these sources to define observable task features, candidate responses,
and labels grounded in recorded observations. Control rules encode executable knowledge, while
supervised adaptation consolidates selected judgments into the model’s parameters. Supervision
can therefore come from direct annotations or from a labelling protocol specified by the reasoning
model, depending on the observable feedback. Reasoning-model supervision has precedents in
distillation [17]; here the supervised targets are structured task judgments.

2.4
Experience, supervision, and training
Training examples pair recorded observations with task-specific supervision. The reasoning
model annotates selected observations or defines a labelling procedure that derives targets from
recorded events and state changes. This converts task knowledge and interaction experience
into training signals without requiring a reward function, expert operational demonstrations,
or a parallel environment simulator. Labels derived from trajectories describe events under
the recorded behaviour; they do not by themselves identify what would have happened under
alternative actions.
The experience pool retains observations, actions, and available outcomes across interaction
rounds. Sampling can emphasize errors, rare states, or task-relevant events, while validation
supports checkpoint selection before redeployment. Supervised objectives train the model’s
structured outputs; class-weighted focal loss [21] is one option when label frequencies are
imbalanced. The sampling distribution, training initialization, and validation partition are
specified for each adaptation round. Repeated or adjacent observations must be distinguished
from independent interaction experience when accounting for the data budget.

Transfer across settings
A fine-tuned decision model can be reused as a candidate module in another setting, with a
different interface or task configuration. Holding the adapter fixed while changing the controller
tests whether its judgments remain useful under the new configuration without additional model
training. Keeping both fixed instead tests direct transfer of the complete system. If both are
updated, the process constitutes joint adaptation rather than direct reuse. Transfer evaluation
accordingly specifies the source and target configurations, parameter updates, controller changes,
and additional interaction budget.
2.7
Online response and offline learning cost
Online response time includes observation, inference, decision, and action submission; offline
learning incurs data collection, labelling, adaptation, and deployment costs. In the intended
serial live-interaction regime, recorded experience is retained for reuse across training iterations.
Prior knowledge informs the reasoning model and controller outside the time-critical execution
path; the trained model supplies online judgments. Separating request latency from the complete
observation-to-action interval is necessary when choosing the online response budget.

Zenless Zone Zero and Black Myth: Wukong provide visual environments in which observations
and action opportunities change during execution. Both implementations combine a 27B decision
model with an OODA controller. The ZZZ study examines model adaptation and subsequent
controller revision across opponents. The Wukong study compares policies for sustained evasion
and offensive action.

Each lane represents an independent execution unit. The three inference workers read the
newest available observation when they start, rather than waiting for the preceding request to
finish. With sufficient service capacity, similarly timed workers can produce results about 𝐿/3

execution timestamps back to the decision worker, so inference duration, decision spacing, and
observation-to-action-completion time remain separate quantities.
The scheduler replaces a pending schedule when a newer one supersedes it and skips a missed
start. A full action queue drops its oldest pending action. These rules bound stale work, but
concurrency is useful only when the serving system has enough capacity; otherwise server-side
queues can erase the intended update frequency. The latency budget therefore concerns the
complete observation-to-execution path rather than model inference alone.

5
Design Properties and Limitations
5.1
Learning and reuse
The framework organizes capability formation around targeted practice guided by informal
language. Prior knowledge identifies what to observe, and interaction supplies task-specific
experience. Adaptation consolidates the resulting distinctions into a decision model.
Learning from limited interaction
Retaining observations across rounds allows the system to reuse costly experience instead of
requiring a new environment instance for every training update. Supervision can focus on errors
and informative states, making the approach suitable for settings in which replication, reset, and
parallel rollout are unavailable. The interaction budget required to acquire a particular capability
remains task dependent.
A compact online decision path
The online controller uses structured decision-model judgments and executable rules, while
extended reasoning and parameter adaptation take place outside the response-critical path.
Learning can encode fine perceptual distinctions in parameters without repeatedly expanding
their verbal specification at every step. Observation processing, serving, and action execution
still determine the achievable response frequency.
Using prior knowledge to guide exploration
Web retrieval, informal human hints, and exploratory actions provide complementary sources of
information about an unfamiliar environment. The reasoning-agent team turns these sources
into initial questions and control rules, then revises them using recorded feedback. This process
directs practice toward relevant distinctions rather than relying exclusively on undirected trial
and error. Using prior knowledge to construct executable skills has related precedents in Code
as Policies and Voyager [7,8].
Targeted model and controller updates
The separation between the decision model and the controller makes the object of an update
explicit. When the judgments are useful but their mapping to actions is inadequate, the controller
can change while the model remains fixed. When perception is inadequate, adaptation can target
the decision model while preserving the control rules. Joint revision is available when the task
requires a different judgment interface.
Reusing the OODA controller
Explicit control rules make dependencies between observations, decisions, and actions inspectable.
Adapting an execution interface or revising a subset of rules can reuse the surrounding control
structure in a new setting. The extent of that reuse depends on which task assumptions and
action semantics remain valid.

17

Reusing learned judgments
A structured decision interface allows an adapted model to be combined with revised questions
and control rules. When its learned distinctions remain applicable, the same parameter set can
support a different controller without additional model training. Such reuse does not establish
that adaptation preserves every unrelated capability; new questions and changed observation
distributions require validation. Policy-dependent state distributions are also central to the
motivation for dataset aggregation [14].
An interpretable learning process
The external-muscle-memory analogy connects deliberate task understanding and targeted
practice under informal verbal guidance. It describes how knowledge becomes available for
action through learning, rather than treating fast inference alone as a sufficient account of skill
acquisition. The explicit interaction and adaptation loops make the sources of subsequent updates
identifiable.
No requirement for expert operational demonstrations
Training observations can be collected by the Rollout / Capture Agents and labelled by the Visual
Labeling Agent through reasoning or event-based protocols. The framework therefore does not
require a human expert to perform the task to generate demonstrations. Public text and images
remain useful sources of knowledge, distinct from a dataset of expert actions.
5.2
Scope and deployment constraints
Task-specific generalization
Learned judgments depend on the visual conditions and state distribution represented in the
experience pool. A change in environment can require revised prompts, control rules, or
additional interaction and adaptation. Reusing an interface or an adapter is a mechanism for
transfer, rather than a guarantee of unchanged performance across settings.
Dependence on external priors
Dispensing with expert operational demonstrations does not eliminate dependence on human
knowledge. Initial questions and control rules rely on descriptions of task structure, available
actions, and relevant state changes. Missing or inaccurate priors can misdirect both exploration
and supervision, making the initial policy and subsequent learning less effective.
Hardware and serving requirements
Low-latency execution depends on model size, observation processing, communication, and
competition for compute resources. Separating environment rendering and inference across
GPUs can reduce contention but increases deployment cost. Resource-limited deployments may
require different model sizes, serving arrangements, or response budgets. The framework does
not impose a universal GPU count or a fixed latency bound.

Supervision and update quality
A recursive learning loop does not ensure that every update improves control. Reasoning-model
annotations can be incorrect, trajectory-derived labels depend on the actions that were taken,
and adjacent observations provide correlated supervision. Model selection can itself overfit a
finite validation set [26]. Future evaluations should separate checkpoint selection from testing
on distinct interaction episodes and retain previous models for comparison.
6
Conclusion
System-One RSI connects task understanding to executable capability through targeted practice
under informal verbal guidance. A team of agents built on reasoning models supplies task
knowledge and control rules and coordinates experience collection, supervision, and training;
the decision model retains learned perceptual judgments in its parameters as external muscle
memory for online OODA execution. The separation between these components supports
targeted model updates, controller revision, and reuse when the relevant task assumptions remain
valid. The framework addresses capability formation in dynamic environments where expert
demonstrations and parallel simulation may be unavailable. The ZZZ and Wukong deployments
illustrate the connection between learned judgments and online action, while small samples
and different experimental settings limit comparative conclusions. Its effectiveness depends on
the quality of prior knowledge and supervision, the coverage of collected experience, and the
resources available for deployment.
