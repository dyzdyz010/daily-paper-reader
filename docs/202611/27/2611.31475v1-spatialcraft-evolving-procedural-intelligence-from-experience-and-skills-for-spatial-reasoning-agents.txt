## Abstract 

Spatial reasoning agents increasingly rely on external tools, such as 3D reconstruction, segmentation, and geometric computation, to answer questions that a multimodal large language model (MLLM) cannot resolve from pixels alone. Tool access, however, shifts the bottleneck to orchestration: the agent must decide which tools to call, in what order, and how to interpret their outputs, yet a stateless agent repeats the same mistakes across tasks. We ask whether a frozen tool-using agent can improve its orchestration from its own interaction history, without parameter updates. We present 

SPATIAL CRAFT , a self-evolving framework that maintains two forms of external knowledge: Experiences , condition-action tactics distilled by contrasting rollouts of the same task, and Skills , multi-step procedures with explicit activa-tion and termination conditions. Three designs couple the two streams. Experiences can be anchored to a Skill, ap-pearing only while it is active rather than once per task. A Contrastive Utility Score estimates each Experience’s effect by randomly withholding it across rollouts of the same task, separating its contribution from task difficulty, and Experi-ences with consistently positive utility are consolidated into the Skills they support. Across five spatial benchmarks and four backbones, SPATIAL CRAFT achieves the best accuracy in all 20 settings, showing that a frozen agent can learn to orchestrate spatial tools from its own experience. ## 1. Introduction 

Spatial reasoning, the ability to infer 3D distances, orien-tations, and object relations from visual observations, is a fundamental prerequisite for embodied agents and robotic manipulation [18, 31, 42, 45, 67]. Yet multimodal large lan-guage models (MLLMs) frequently fail at complex spatial queries when relying solely on internal parametric knowl-

> *Corresponding author.

edge [8, 10, 13, 18, 31, 35, 42, 54, 60]. Consequently, a growing line of work equips MLLMs with external spa-tial tools, such as 3D reconstruction, metric depth estima-tion, and pose estimation, enabling them to explicitly com-pute metric geometric quantities rather than relying on un-grounded visual perception [11, 15, 16, 40, 41, 45, 80]. However, tool integration shifts the bottleneck from per-ception to orchestration. A tool-augmented agent must de-cide which tools to call, in what order, and how to interpret their outputs. Because these decisions are interdependent, a single tactical error can derail the entire reasoning chain, yet most spatial agents operate statelessly, discarding trial-and-error insights after each episode. Supervised fine-tuning or reinforcement learning can internalize spatial and tool-use competence [8, 10, 11, 36, 38, 50, 77, 80], but must be re-peated whenever the backbone or toolset changes. An al-ternative is parameter-update-free self-evolution, in which a frozen agent accumulates knowledge in an external mem-ory [53, 63, 73, 74, 76]. SMA [72] shows that such mem-ory benefits tool-free spatial reasoning. For tool-augmented agents, however, useful knowledge spans both multi-step tool procedures and step-level tactics, raising the challenge of how to structure, validate, and integrate it without accu-mulating spurious or redundant heuristics. To address this challenge, we draw on the hierarchical nature of tool use, which prior work captures with two com-plementary forms of reusable knowledge [32]: procedural 

Skills , which orchestrate multi-step tool pipelines (e.g., re-constructing a scene before querying distances) [46, 57, 63, 78], and tactical Experiences , which guide localized deci-sions (e.g., re-prompting a segmentation model upon detect-ing a failure) [47, 55, 76]. In spatial reasoning, the two are intertwined, as a tactic’s usefulness depends on the proce-dural stage: re-prompting segmentation helps during object grounding but is irrelevant during metric comparison. This suggests structuring knowledge hierarchically, with tacti-cal Experiences anchored to the procedural Skills they sup-port. Tactics that prove consistently effective can then be arXiv:2611.31475v1 [cs.AI] 23 Nov 2026 absorbed into the procedure itself, so that the Skill evolves and the Experience bank stays compact, much as human ex-pertise compiles practiced rules into procedures [1]. Beyond structural organization, a self-evolving agent re-quires empirical validation of its accumulated knowledge. MLLMs often distill lessons that are linguistically plausible but practically misleading [29], and retaining such untested heuristics lets the memory accumulate errors alongside competence. Correlating a lesson with successful episodes, as in reward-based memory valuation [72, 74], is insuffi-cient because the correlation is confounded by task diffi-culty: heuristics retrieved for inherently easier tasks may appear effective regardless of their actual impact. Isolating the utility of a lesson instead requires a controlled compar-ison. By randomly showing an Experience in only a sub-set of the parallel rollouts of the same task [52], the agent can contrast their outcomes to isolate the effect of the tactic from task difficulty, at no extra interaction cost, since these rollouts are already collected to distill Experiences. Building on these insights, we present S PATIAL CRAFT ,a parameter-update-free framework that co-evolves tactical Experiences and procedural Skills for tool-augmented spa-tial agents. During exploration, S PATIAL CRAFT leverages multi-trajectory rollouts to distill Experiences, anchoring each strictly to the active Skill. The framework evaluates each Experience using a Contrastive Utility Score, which estimates its contribution by comparing within-task rollouts with and without the tactic. Experiences that systemati-cally improve outcomes are consolidated into their anchor Skills, while ineffective ones are retired. Skills themselves are refined through trajectory feedback, with proposed revi-sions rigorously verified against prior decision states before adoption. During deployment, the accumulated dual-level knowledge base is frozen, enabling the agent to seamlessly retrieve task-level insights and trigger context-aware tactics precisely when the corresponding Skill is invoked, so tactics surface where they apply. Experiments across five representative spatial bench-marks and four base VLMs show that S PATIAL CRAFT con-sistently outperforms tool-only and memory-based base-lines, demonstrating that evolving procedural knowledge provides an effective parameter-update-free path for spatial self-evolution. Our core contributions are three-fold: 

❶ We propose S PATIAL CRAFT , a parameter-update-free framework that organizes tactical Experiences around the task-level Skills they support and co-evolves both, enabling frozen spatial reasoning agents to transform verified inter-action into reusable procedural intelligence. 

❷ We introduce the Contrastive Utility Score, which ran-domizes each Experience’s exposure across rollouts of the same task to estimate its effect separately from task diffi-culty, together with a judge-based verification that accepts Skill revisions by replaying recorded decision states and re-quires no token likelihoods, suiting proprietary MLLMs. 

❸ We evaluate SPATIAL CRAFT across diverse spatial benchmarks and VLM backbones, demonstrating consistent performance gains together with effective in-domain, cross-task, and cross-agent knowledge reuse.

## 3. Experiments 

3.1. Experimental Setup 

Benchmarks. We evaluate on five spatial benchmarks: RoboSpatial [54] and ERQA [56] for embodied scene and robot reasoning, Omni3D-Bench [45] for metric 3D queries, SAT [50] for egocentric and two-frame dynamics, and ViewSpatial [35] for multi-perspective orientation. All results are reported on Ddep . Benchmark details and split statistics are given in Appendix B.1. 

Implementation. We use four backbones: GPT-5.4, GPT-5.4-mini, Qwen3.6-27B, and Qwen3.5-9B, with open-weight models served by vLLM [33] in non-thinking mode. In each setting, Mexec , Mkb , and the Judge share the same backbone. Accumulation runs N = 4 rollouts per task with temperature 0.7 and top-p 0.9; deployment uses tempera-ture 0. Each call is capped at 16,384 output tokens and each trajectory at 50 steps. Retrieval and Skill activation use 

text-embedding-3-large . We set B = 6 , Nc = 3 ,

Np = 2 , λ = 2 , nmin = 3 , and τg = 0 .2; the remaining detailed hyperparameters are listed in Appendix B.2. 

Baselines. We compare S PATIAL CRAFT with seven base-lines: CoT, Tools-only ReAct [70], RAG [34], MemP [20], MemRL-R [74], MemRL-GT and SMA [72]. Since the original MemRL uses reward-only reflection, we implement MemRL-GT, which provides ground-truth answers during reflection for fair comparison. Baselines do not receive the seed Skills. Further details are specified in Appendix B.

3.2. Main Results 

SpatialCraft consistently helps agents translate tool ac-cess into effective spatial reasoning. As shown in Table 1, SpatialCraft improves macro-averaged accuracy over Tool-Only by 7.6–22.6 percentage points across four backbones, and exceeds the strongest baseline SMA by 2.3–4.2 points. This benefit is especially evident with Qwen3.5-9B: tool ac-cess alone lowers average accuracy from 52.4% to 50.4%, whereas SpatialCraft achieves 59.0%. These results suggest that reusable experience and procedural skills provide use-ful guidance for exploiting spatial tools, including when the backbone struggles to benefit from tool access alone. 

SpatialCraft is particularly effective when spatial judg-ments depend on 3D geometry and reference frames. On Omni3D, which requires reasoning about three-dimensional quantities and relations, SpatialCraft reaches 50.7% and 46.0% accuracy with GPT-5.4 and GPT-5.4-mini, exceed-ing SMA by 8.3 and 7.6 percentage points. On ViewS-patial, which evaluates localization from different perspec-tives, GPT-5.4-mini achieves 62.6%, compared with SMA’s 55.9%. Together, these gains are consistent with the role of learned guidance in linking spatial evidence to the ge-ometric and viewpoint constraints posed by the question. Appendix E displays case examples that trace step-by-step tool use on queries alongside baseline trajectories.

3.7. Experiences Enable Flexible Orchestration 

We investigate whether Experiences also change which 

tools the agent calls, comparing tool-usage shares on Ro-boSpatial and Omni3D with GPT-5.4-mini . Shares count all task-execution calls with valid tool names, exclud-ing internal knowledge operations (More in Appendix D). 

Experiences steer tool selection toward what each task requires. Adding Experiences shifts tool usage in a task-dependent direction (Fig. 6). On RoboSpatial, the combined share of SAM3 and Mask rises from 36.1% to 44.7% with-out Skills and from 37.1% to 46.7% with them, while De-tect declines, favoring precise object regions over bounding boxes. On Omni3D, Geometry rises from 26.3% to 32.4% and from 27.9% to 35.0%, along with Reconstruct, favoring explicit 3D computation. Since the shift appears regardless of Skills, it stems from the Experiences themselves. 

## 4. Related Work 

Spatial reasoning with MLLMs. Spatial reasoning in MLLMs has been advanced by post-training on spatial data or geometric environments [8, 10, 13, 36, 50, 77], and by tool-augmented agents that invoke 3D reconstruction and geometry modules to compute spatial quantities explic-itly [11, 15, 16, 41, 45, 80]. Most tool-augmented agents re-main stateless, discarding lessons from one task to the next. 

Self-evolving agents. Parameter-update-free agents in-stead accumulate external knowledge from interaction, as reflective insights [47, 53, 55, 76], reusable workflows [20, 63], or procedural skills [46, 57, 78], and recent work com-bines action-level experiences with task-level skills [32]. To decide what to retain, these systems rely on LLM judg-ment [32], token-likelihood ratios [46], or the rewards of episodes in which an entry is retrieved [72, 74]; the last is confounded by task difficulty, as entries retrieved mostly for easy tasks appear useful regardless of their effect. A full discussion of related work is given in Appendix F. 5. Conclusion 

We presented S PATIAL CRAFT , which lets a frozen spatial agent improve tool orchestration by anchoring Experiences to the Skills they support, validating them through random-ized within-task contrast, and folding proven ones into those Skills. Empirically, it achieves the best accuracy in all 20 benchmark-backbone settings. Our results suggest that, as tools grow more capable, the decisive knowledge lies less in what an agent knows than in knowing when to apply it, and in keeping only what has been shown to help.

## B. Experimental Setup 

B.1. Benchmarks and Splits 

Benchmarks. RoboSpatial [54] poses three question types on single indoor images: context questions ask for im-age points marking free space that satisfies a spatial relation, while configuration and compatibility questions ask yes/no whether two objects stand in a given relation or whether an object fits a target location. All memory-based baselines use the same backbone,

## C. Analysis Protocols 

Unless stated otherwise, analyses use GPT-5.4-mini ,the settings of Table 7, and greedy single-run deployment on Ddep . Results are averaged over three independent accu-mulation runs. 

C.1. Component Ablations 

Table 2 evaluates RoboSpatial and Omni3D-Bench; each benchmark accumulates on its own Denv . Variants fall into two groups. 

Deployment-time variants. These reuse the three knowl-edge bases of the full model and disable one stream only at deployment. • w/o Experiences : neither global nor anchored Experi-ences are shown; Skill activation is unchanged. • w/o Skills : no Skill is activated. Anchored Experiences join E∅ and are retrieved and rewritten as global ones. 

Accumulation-time variants. These disable one mecha-nism, re-run accumulation with the same task order, and deploy the resulting knowledge base. • w/o Anchoring : the critique sets αe = ∅ for every Ex-perience, so all Experiences are retrieved once per task. Because graduation requires an anchor, consolidation is also inactive; this drop therefore upper-bounds the effect of anchoring alone. • w/o Consolidation : the lifecycle is unchanged, but ⊕ re-ceives Gk = ∅, so graduated Experiences stay in the bank as anchored Experiences. • w/o CUS : Mkb decides retirement, graduation, and evic-tion without outcome contrasts. • w/o Skill Evolution : Φ is disabled and the pool keeps the six seeds. Experiences are still anchored to seeds, and graduated ones remain in the bank. • w/o Decomposition Retrieval : global Experiences are retrieved with the full query as a single retrieval query, with the number retrieved matched to the full model’s av-erage. • w/o Experience Rewrite : retrieved global Experiences are shown verbatim; eligibility for randomized exposure then follows retrieval alone.

C.5. Tool-Use Error Analysis 

We deploy the same frozen knowledge base on RoboSpa-tial. Skill Only disables Experiences; Experience Only dis-ables Skills, so that anchored Experiences are retrieved as global ones, matching the deployment-time variants in Ap-pendix C.1. 

Attempts and categories. An attempt is any model out-put submitted to the tool-call interface. Attempts are recorded before parsing, final answers are excluded, and each retry counts as a new attempt. A failed attempt is as-signed to the first check it fails: • Format : the output cannot be parsed into a valid call, e.g ., malformed JSON, missing call fields, or several calls in one step. • Resolution : the call is well formed but names an unregis-tered tool. Choosing an existing but unsuitable tool is not counted. • Validation : the tool resolves, but its arguments fail pre-execution checks, e.g ., missing, ill-typed, or out-of-range arguments, or references to nonexistent artifacts. • Execution : the call passes all checks but the tool raises an exception, times out, or returns an explicit failure. A valid empty result, such as no detected object, is not a failure. Each rate divides the number of failures in its category by the total number of attempts, so all four categories share one denominator. Failures outside the tools, such as API errors and job interruptions, are excluded. 

Efficiency. Average tool calls is the number of attempts per task. The exact repeat rate is the fraction of attempts that match an earlier attempt in tool and normalized argu-ments on the same input state, with no relevant state change in between.

D.2. Diagnosing the Contrastive Utility Score 

Sec. 2.3 argues that valuing an Experience by the rewards of the episodes in which it appears is confounded by task difficulty, and that randomized exposure removes this con-found. We test this claim directly on RoboSpatial with 

GPT-5.4-mini , using three diagnostics: (i) whether each score tracks task difficulty, (ii) how the Experience lifecy-cle evolves during accumulation, and (iii) whether the score identifies the Experiences that carry the accuracy gain at deployment. Diagnostics (i) and (ii) are computed from the accumulation logs and require no additional rollouts; (iii) changes only the deployed knowledge base. 

Setup. For (i), we consider every Experience that com-pleted probation ( ne ≥ nmin ). Each receives two scores computed from the same accumulation rollouts. The naive score is the mean reward of the eligible rollouts in which e

was shown, which corresponds to the reward-based valua-tion of SMA [72] and MemRL [74] without retrieval-time re-ranking. For the Table 2 ablation, we replace U (e) with the naive score minus the global mean reward during ac-cumulation, keeping randomized exposure and all lifecycle thresholds unchanged. The second is U (e), taken when the state of e was last decided. As a difficulty proxy that shares no rollouts with either score, we run the memory-free Re-Act agent three times on every task of Denv and average its success over the tasks on which e was eligible. Using the withheld rollouts instead would correlate spuriously with 

U (e), since δi(e) subtracts their outcomes. Reward-based valuation tracks task difficulty; the Con-trastive Utility Score does not. Fig. 8(a) shows that the naive score rises with the ReAct success rate on the tasks where an Experience was eligible: an Experience retrieved mostly for easy tasks scores high regardless of its own effect. U (e) is nearly uncorrelated with the same proxy (Fig. 8b), because each contrast compares rollouts of the same task. The two scores rank Experiences differently (Spearman ρ = 0 .46 ). Of the 155 Experiences retired by CUS, 59 (38%) score above the median naive value, so a reward-based valuation would have kept them. 

Most distilled Experiences are retired, and the bank stays compact. Of the 239 Experiences created during ac-cumulation, 155 (65%) are retired, 41 graduate (39 of them already absorbed into their anchor Skills), 31 are retained, and 12 remain in probation (Fig. 8c). The bank peaks at 68 entries and ends at 45 while creation continues, so retire-ment and consolidation, rather than the capacity limit, keep it compact. The high retirement rate is consistent with our motivation of CUS: many lessons that read as plausible do not improve outcomes once their exposure is randomized. 

The score identifies the Experiences that carry the gain. 

Table 9 shows that removing the top 20% of Experiences by 

U (e) costs 4.6 points on RoboSpatial, nearly half of the 9.6 points lost by removing all of them, and more than twice the cost of removing a random 20%. Removing the top 20% by naive score costs about as much as random removal, so the naive ranking carries little information about which Experi-ences matter. Removing the bottom 20% by U (e) leaves ac-curacy unchanged, and restoring retired Experiences lowers it, confirming that retirement discards entries that are use-less or harmful. Omni3D-Bench shows the same ordering.

Table 3 reports the size and deployment cost of the knowl-edge base accumulated by GPT-5.4-mini on RoboSpa-tial. Here we report the composition of every knowledge base used in Table 1 (Table 12) and the cost of build-ing it (Table 13). Stored tokens are counted with the 

cl100k base tokenizer as in Appendix C.4. Accumula-tion tokens are those reported by the API, including image tokens, and cover every call made while processing Denv .

The knowledge base stays small as accumulation grows. 

Every final knowledge base holds 9–16 Skills and 41–96 Experiences, totaling 5.3k–11.4k stored tokens (Table 12). Even the largest, accumulated over the 2,856 tasks of ViewSpatial, is smaller than the 22.5k and 39.2k tokens that MemP and SMA store for RoboSpatial alone (Table 3). ViewSpatial has 16 times as many accumulation tasks as RoboSpatial but only a 1.9 times larger knowledge base, because retirement and consolidation remove Experiences about as fast as new ones are created once the common question types are covered. 

Most Experiences leave the bank, mostly through retire-ment. Across benchmarks, 63–82% of created Experi-ences are retired and 14–19% are absorbed into Skills. The rest remain in the bank, about half anchored and half global. ViewSpatial retires the largest share (82%): its long accu-mulation repeatedly produces near-duplicate lessons about the same viewpoint questions, which add no contrast once an equivalent entry exists. Omni3D-Bench has the highest graduation rate (19.0%) and the longest Skills (242 tokens), consistent with its multi-step metric procedures, which offer more steps into which a tactic can be folded. 

Weaker backbones keep more Experiences and gradu-ate fewer. On RoboSpatial, the graduation rate falls from 19.9% with GPT-5.4 to 6.0% with Qwen3.5-9B , while the bank grows from 41 to 61 Experiences and the pool shrinks from 13 to 9 Skills. A weaker executor follows a shown Experience less consistently, which shrinks its con-trast, so fewer Experiences reach τg and more remain as separate prompts instead of being folded into Skills. This agrees with Fig. 2(c), where Qwen3.5-9B gains least from accumulation, and with Appendix D.1, where it benefits from a knowledge base distilled by a stronger backbone. Half of the accumulation cost is rollouts that every base-line also pays. SPATIAL CRAFT spends 604k tokens per accumulation task, about 106M tokens over RoboSpatial’s 175 tasks (Table 13). Half of this (51.1%) comes from the 

N =4 rollouts, which all memory-based baselines also run. The Contrastive Utility Score adds no calls, since it reuses the outcomes of these rollouts. Experience accumulation adds two calls per task (10.3% of tokens). Skill evolution accounts for the remaining 38.6%, most of it from key-state replay and the Judge (26.3% together). This is the price of verifying revisions without token likelihoods, so that the same procedure applies to proprietary backbones. Remov-ing it (w/o Candidate Verification) would save about a quar-ter of the tokens but lets half of the accepted revisions fail to help (Table 11). 

The extra cost is paid once, before deployment. Over-all, accumulation costs 1.9–2.2 × that of the baselines per task. The cost is incurred once per benchmark and back-bone, and knowledge is frozen afterwards. At deployment, Skills and Experiences together add 555.9 prompt tokens per step (209.3 and 346.6), fewer than MemP (601.8) and SMA (632.6; Table 3), so the per-step cost does not grow despite the richer knowledge. The same knowledge base can also be reused across tasks and executors without fur-ther accumulation (Appendix D.1).

## F. Full Related Work 

Spatial reasoning benchmarks. A growing set of bench-marks probes the spatial abilities of MLLMs. BLINK [21] shows that models that describe images fluently still fail at perceptual tasks such as relative depth and correspondence, and 3DSRBench [42], OmniSpatial [31], SITE [60], and VSI-Bench [67] extend evaluation to 3D, multi-view, and video settings. Other benchmarks isolate specific capabil-ities: perspective taking in ViewSpatial [35], robot-centric relations in RoboSpatial [54] and EmbSpatial-Bench [19], embodied reasoning in ERQA [56], dynamic and ego-centric reasoning in SAT [50], and metric 3D queries in Omni3D-Bench [45]. Earlier work on 3D and embodied question answering [4, 17, 43, 44] and embodied agent benchmarks [69] assesses spatial understanding inside re-constructed or simulated scenes. We evaluate on RoboSpa-tial, ERQA, Omni3D-Bench, SAT, and ViewSpatial, which together cover robot-centric, embodied, metric, dynamic, and perspective reasoning. 

Training MLLMs for spatial reasoning. One line of work equips models with 3D inputs, encoding point clouds or scene features directly [26, 30, 61, 65, 81]. Another post-trains 2D MLLMs on synthesized spatial question-answer data [8, 10, 13, 18, 50]. Recent methods close the loop be-tween data and model, deriving training signals from de-terministic geometric environments [36], from the consis-tency of geometric logic [38], or from an iterated data-model pipeline [77], and whether explicit object localiza-tion is needed for spatial reasoning at all is itself under study [40]. These approaches improve the backbone, so they must be repeated whenever the backbone or toolset changes. SPATIAL CRAFT keeps the backbone frozen and instead improves how it uses existing tools. Tool-augmented spatial agents. Vision foundation mod-els now provide reliable geometric primitives: monocu-lar and multi-view geometry [6, 27, 37, 59, 68], open-vocabulary detection and segmentation [9, 39], and object orientation [62]. Following tool-using language agents [51, 70], spatial agents compose these modules into explicit computations. VADAR [45] lets the agent synthesize new API functions for 3D queries, pySpatial [41] generates 3D visual programs zero-shot, and GCA [12] first formalizes a query as geometric constraints and then solves it under them. SpaceTools [11] and S-Agent [16] train the pol-icy to call spatial tools, SpatialClaw [15] redesigns the ac-tion interface through which the agent operates, and Spa-tialCLI [80] learns to reason with spatial tools and then without them. General multimodal agents follow the same trend [23, 25], and dedicated benchmarks evaluate tool-enabled visual reasoning [3, 22]. These agents either re-quire training or approach each task anew. S PATIAL CRAFT 

adds a knowledge base that a frozen agent accumulates across tasks, recording when and how to orchestrate the same tools. 

Memory and self-evolving agents. LLM agents extend their context with external memory, through hierarchi-cal memory management [48, 71], long-term memory stores [14, 66, 79], retrieval-augmented generation [7, 34], and the memory streams of generative agents [49]; surveys summarize this design space [24, 28, 75]. Self-evolving agents use such memory to improve without parameter up-dates. They store reflective insights [47, 53, 55, 76] or evolving context playbooks [73], induce reusable work-flows [20, 63], and build libraries of procedural skills, writ-ten as executable code [57, 64, 78] or as natural-language procedures [2, 46]. XSkill [32] combines action-level experiences with task-level skills in multimodal agents, and multimodal memory has been developed for visual skills [58] and semantic knowledge [5]. Closest to our set-ting, SMA [72] accumulates experience-grounded procedu-ral memory for spatial reasoning without tools. S PATIAL -CRAFT differs in how the two forms of knowledge interact: an Experience is anchored to the Skill it serves, is injected only while that Skill is active, and, once proven useful, is consolidated into its procedure. G.1. tools.Reconstruct tools.Reconstruct is a client wrapper for the per-ception serving role. Its single entry method calls Depth Anything 3 [37] on a frame batch and returns a 

Reconstruction object that exposes per-frame depth, camera intrinsics, camera-to-world extrinsics, a dense per-frame point map in world coordinates, and a top-down ren-dering helper function. The output coordinate system is gravity-aligned with respect to the first camera: +Y points up, the ground plane sits at Y ≈ 0, and the first camera looks toward −Z. The output spatial resolution matches the input frame resolution, so masks from tools.SAM3 

can be combined directly with the reconstructed point maps without resizing. Table 14 summarizes the interface. 

G.2. tools.SAM3 tools.SAM3 is a client wrapper for the perception serv-ing role and exposes single-frame and video-mode seg-mentation against SAM3 [9], together with an object-existence check. All segmentation methods return a

PerFrameMask whose absolute video frame indices align with those of any Reconstruction the agent has built. Table 15 summarizes the image-mode methods, existence check, and mask interface. Each image-mode method oper-ates on a single PIL image and returns masks for one frame. 

G.3. tools.Geometry tools.Geometry is a static class that provides the nu-merical primitives most commonly needed for spatial rea-soning over reconstructed scenes. All methods operate on 

numpy arrays. Table 16 summarizes their inputs and out-puts. 

G.4. tools.Mask tools.Mask is a static class that provides standard mask statistics over the boolean mask arrays returned by 

tools.SAM3 . All inputs are 2D numpy arrays with shape 

(H, W ) unless otherwise noted. Table 17 summarizes the interface. 

G.5. tools.Draw tools.Draw is a static class that produces PIL-based an-notation overlays. All methods accept a numpy (H, W, 3) 

uint8 array, a PIL image, or an InputImages[i] en-try, and return a PIL image of the same size; the input image is never modified. Colours may be passed as an (R, G, B) uint8 tuple or as any matplotlib or CSS4 colour name. Each method accepts either a single primitive or a list of primitives, with a matching list of colours when per-primitive colouring is desired. Table 18 summarizes the drawing methods. 

G.6. tools.Detect tools.Detect is a proposed client wrapper for an open-vocabulary detector such as GroundingDINO [39]. It complements vlm.locate and text-prompted SAM3 segmentation by exposing detector-native boxes, labels, and confidence scores without requiring a separate VLM session. The output boxes use pixel-space xyxy co-ordinates and can be passed directly to tools.SAM3. segment image by box . Table 19 summarizes the pro-posed interface and its composition with SAM3. 

G.7. tools.Pose tools.Pose is a proposed object-centric pose tool that combines a reconstructed object point set with a single-image semantic orientation estimator such as Orient Any-thing [62]. It estimates a semantic object frame rather than only an unoriented 3D bounding box. Following GCA [12], the local origin is placed at the robust centroid of the masked object points, +Zobj points toward the ob-ject’s semantic front, +Yobj points toward its semantic bot-tom, and +Xobj follows the right-hand rule. This object frame enables perspective-taking and reference-frame rea-soning such as “left of the chair from the chair’s viewpoint.” Tables 20 and 21 summarize the proposed interface and re-turned object. 

G.8. tools.Scale tools.Scale is an optional client wrapper for metric-scale verification with a monocular metric-geometry model such as MoGe-2 [59]. Its purpose is to estimate a multi-plicative correction factor and model disagreement, rather than to assume that the original reconstruction is scale-free. Table 22 summarizes the interface and explicit correction. 

When to apply the correction. The correction should be applied only when the cross-model disagreement is accept-able and the task requires absolute metric quantities.
